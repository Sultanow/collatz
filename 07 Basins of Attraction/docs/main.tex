\begin{abstract}
We study why Collatz-type maps with similar local predecessor statistics can have different global cycles and basin shares. The maps $3x+1$ and $3x-1$ have corresponding local branching and cotree statistics, yet the latter has three known positive cycles. The classical parity-word divisibility criterion gives an exhaustive census of $1097$ primitive cycles for $3x+c$ with $|c|\le1000$ and parity-word length at most $40$. Using first-passage estimates of Gon\c{c}alves, Greenfeld and Madrid, we prove that every completely invariant set of the accelerated map $T_{3,c}$, for odd $c\ge-1$, has a logarithmic density. In particular, basin and predecessor-set densities exist in this sense. Conditionally on a recent natural-density transport of Allikvere, which carries over to all these maps, they are natural densities. The resulting predecessor potential $h(a)$ satisfies an exact conservation law across the backward levels of a cycle. Gcd classes and multiplicative cosets give further invariant sets of exactly computable density, but do not by themselves determine individual basin densities. We also prove a residue-class \emph{lower bound} for the means of $a\,h(a)$ in terms of the Syracuse law; a matching upper bound and the proposed exact profile remain conjectural. Numerically, a parameter-free harmonic backward-mass model predicts $410$ measured basin shares with approximately $3\,\%$ median relative error. Additional conclusions for $3x\pm1$ use recent predecessor-density preprints of Mazur and El Jaouhari. Lean~4 checks the elementary finite statements; the density and Syracuse statements are not machine-certified.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%=====================================================================
Crandall \cite{Crandall_1978} generalized the $3x+1$ sequence by introducing the function $f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:func_qxplus1}
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
qx+1	&	2\nmid x,\\
x/2		&	\text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
for odd $q\geq 3$. More generally, for odd $q\ge1$ and odd $c$ we consider $x\mapsto qx+c$. Since every odd step is followed by at least one halving, we work with the accelerated map
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:T}
T_{q,c}(x)=
\begin{cases}
(qx+c)/2	&	2\nmid x,\\
x/2		&	\text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
which has the same cycles as~\eqref{eq:func_qxplus1} (for $c=1$), up to the omitted even members. We call a ``$qx+c$ cycle'' a sequence of distinct positive integers $(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)$ with $T_{q,c}(x_i)=x_{i+1}$ and $T_{q,c}(x_n)=x_1$.

Leslie Davison observed that \emph{circuits}, i.e.\ cycles consisting of one run of odd steps followed by one run of halvings, correspond one-to-one to the solutions $(h,k,l)$ of the Diophantine equation
\begin{equation}
(2^{k+l}-3^k)h=2^l-1,
\label{eq:diophantine_davison}
\end{equation}
see \cite[p.~67]{Lagarias_2010}. Steiner \cite{Steiner_1977} proved that $(h,k,l)=(1,1,1)$ is the only solution, so the trivial cycle is the only circuit of $3x+1$. Simons and de Weger \cite{Simons_deWeger_2005} and Hercher \cite{Hercher_2023} extended this to $m$-cycles (cycles with $m$ runs of odd steps) for $m\le 68$ and $m\le 91$, respectively. The general cycle problem remains open; convergence has been verified for all $n<2^{71}$ \cite{Barina_2021,Barina_2025}.

For $q>3$ cycles are rare. Table~\ref{table:known_cycles} lists the non-trivial cycles of~\eqref{eq:func_qxplus1} for odd $q\le1000$.

Let $(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n)$ be the odd members of a cycle of~\eqref{eq:func_qxplus1} in cyclic order. Then the product
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:condition_cycle_existance}
2^\alpha=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(q+\frac{1}{a_i}\right)
\end{equation}
is a power of two, see for example \cite[p.~327]{Lagarias_2010}. Here $\alpha$ is the number of divisions by $2$ in one period, and $n$ is the number of odd members.

\subsection*{Related work}
Cycles of the maps $3x+d$ have been tabulated before. Lagarias \cite{Lagarias_1990} introduced primitive cycles, whose members are coprime to $d$, and conjectured that every map $3x+d$ has at least one and only finitely many of them. Belaga and Mignotte \cite{Belaga_Mignotte_2000,Belaga_Mignotte_2006} listed the primitive cycles for all $d<20000$, completely as they argue, and Morley \cite{OEIS_A226607} confirmed these lists by following every trajectory with starting value below $10^8$. Belaga and Mignotte also embedded the $3x+1$ conjecture in the $3x+d$ context \cite{Belaga_Mignotte_1998} and described the cycles of all maps $3x+d$ at once, through a duality between the shift $d$ and the members of a cycle \cite{Belaga_Mignotte_DMTCS_2006}. Wegner \cite{Wegner_2021} listed the positive and negative cycles of $3x+k$ for $k\le9997$ reached from starting values $|x_0|\le2\cdot10^7$, using the reduction by common factors of $x$ and $k$. Cox \cite{Cox_2012} modelled the number of cycles per $d$ by a Poisson distribution. These lists are conjectural: a cycle whose basin contains no small starting value would be missed. The census of Section~\ref{sec:zoo} is complete by construction for bounded word length, and it confirms the published lists in that range.

Densities enter through predecessor sets. Wirsching \cite{Wirsching_1998} studied the density of the set of integers that iterate to a fixed integer under $3x+1$ with $3$-adic methods. Tao \cite{Tao_2022} proved that almost all orbits attain almost bounded values, in the sense of logarithmic density. Wegner \cite{Wegner_2021} observed that the oscillation in Tao's mixing estimate is the same for all maps $3x+k$ with $k$ coprime to $6$, and Gon\c{c}alves, Greenfeld and Madrid \cite{GGM_2025} extended Tao's theorem to a class of maps that contains $3x+c$ for odd $c\ge-1$. They also observed numerically that the three cycles of $3x-1$ attract about a third of all integers each, and asked whether such densities exist \cite[Remark~1.7]{GGM_2025}; their question concerns natural densities. Mazur \cite{Mazur_2026} recently upgraded Tao's theorem to natural density, with an explicit logarithmic time bound, by comparing the first-passage laws of natural and logarithmic counting; Allikvere \cite{Allikvere_2026} independently obtained a natural-density analogue of Tao's theorem. Mazur also proved that a positive lower density of the integers reaches $1$ \cite{Mazur_PD_2026} and that, for every $a$ not divisible by $3$, the integers whose orbit reaches $a$ have positive lower natural density \cite{Mazur_Pred_2026}; El Jaouhari \cite{ElJaouhari_2026} proved the analogue for $3x-1$ and deduced Conjecture~A of Applegate and Lagarias. A recent Lean formalisation \cite{CLT_2026} states that these predecessor sets of $3x+1$ have natural densities, together with central limit theorems for the hitting times. All these results concern $3x\pm1$; Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} below is in logarithmic density, but holds for every map $3x+c$ with odd $c\ge-1$.

On the local side, Kleinnijenhuis, Kleinnijenhuis and Aydogan \cite{Kleinnijenhuis_2026} develop a graph-theoretic approach to the $an+b$ conjectures. They represent these maps by Cayley trees with Leftward and Upward edges and three-colour pictures, with a detailed congruence-class bookkeeping that includes cotree densities. Section~\ref{sec:local} adopts this tree perspective.

\subsection*{Two views of the same maps}
These maps admit two descriptions. The \emph{local} view starts from the trivial cycle and grows the tree of all predecessors, organised by congruence classes. It describes how numbers branch, and it is the same for every map in the family: by $x\mapsto -x$, the $3x-1$ map on the positive integers is the $3x+1$ map on the negative integers, which has the three cycles through $-1$, $-5$ and $-17$. Both maps produce the same branching statistics (Proposition~\ref{prop:local}). In the language of Plato's cave, they cast identical shadows. Density arguments built on this view reach ``almost all'' statements \cite{Terras_1976,Tao_2022}, but they cannot decide individual orbits.

The \emph{global} view encodes a cycle by its parity word and asks whether the corresponding rational number is a positive integer. This is decided by the arithmetic of $2^l-q^k$, and it is exactly what separates $3x+1$ from $3x-1$.

The guiding question of this paper is therefore: \emph{what determines the global dynamics of a Collatz-type map when its local predecessor statistics agree with those of another map?} Sections~\ref{sec:local}--\ref{sec:basins} give three complementary answers. The local congruence and branching statistics compared here agree for $3x+1$ and $3x-1$. Cycles are characterised by an exact global divisibility condition. Logarithmic basin densities exist throughout the stated family, but the values vary and are largely unexplained.

\subsection*{Main results}
For $A\subseteq\N$ let $\delta(A)=\lim_{N\to\infty}(\log N)^{-1}\sum_{n<N,\,n\in A}1/n$ be its logarithmic density, if the limit exists. Our main theoretical result separates the existence of basin densities from their values. Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} states that every set of positive integers that is completely invariant under $T_{3,c}$, with odd $c\ge-1$, has a logarithmic density; in particular, every basin of attraction has one. Existence is therefore common to all maps of the family, whereas the values separate them: numerically, the three basins of $3x-1$ have nearly equal densities, while two cycles of the same type in $3x+5$ attract about $50\,\%$ and $9\,\%$ of all integers. For the Collatz map itself, Corollary~\ref{cor:collatz} shows that the positive integers whose orbit reaches $1$ form a set with a logarithmic density. This does not decide the Collatz conjecture, which asserts that the set is $\N$, but it shows unconditionally that the logarithmic proportion of these integers converges. By a recent natural-counting transport estimate of Mazur \cite{Mazur_2026}, the density is even a natural density (Corollary~\ref{cor:natural}), and by \cite{Mazur_PD_2026} it is positive. The independent transport of Allikvere \cite{Allikvere_2026} carries over to every map $3x+c$; conditionally on it, every completely invariant set and every predecessor set of these maps has a natural density (Theorem~\ref{thm:natural}).

The proof is short. A basin is not changed by the first passage of an orbit below a threshold, so the stabilisation of first passages of Gon\c{c}alves, Greenfeld and Madrid \cite{GGM_2025}, after Tao \cite{Tao_2022}, makes the basin shares of consecutive logarithmic windows converge; two windows of incommensurable ratio then exclude oscillations in $\log\log N$. For $3x+1$, the existence of natural densities of predecessor sets has recently been stated in formalised form \cite{CLT_2026}; Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} covers the whole family $3x+c$, in logarithmic density. The same argument gives every predecessor set a logarithmic density $h(a)$, and the density of a basin is the sum of $h$ over every level of the backward tree of its cycle (Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}). With the recent positivity results for $3x\pm1$ \cite{Mazur_Pred_2026,ElJaouhari_2026}, every nonempty completely invariant set of these two maps has positive density (Corollary~\ref{cor:dichotomy}); for $3x-1$, the three known cycles attract all integers if and only if their basin densities add up to $1$. How to compute the values from the global dynamics remains the central open problem (Problem~\ref{prob:basins}). Section~\ref{sec:basins} gives two partial answers. An elementary multiplicative invariant gives exact densities for certain invariant residue-class unions, not automatically for their constituent basins (Proposition~\ref{prop:cosets}); for a prime $p\equiv\pm1\pmod{24}$, for example, the Legendre symbol $(n/p)$ is constant along the orbits of $3x+p$. And the harmonic mass of the backward tree of a cycle, a heuristic quantity computed from the cycle alone, predicts the measured densities of $410$ cycles to about $5\,\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:predictor}).

The unifying object is the \emph{predecessor potential} $h(a)$.
For a cycle $\mathcal C$, Proposition~\ref{prop:potential} gives the exact
conservation law
\[
  \delta(\mathcal C)=\sum_{x\in L_d(\mathcal C)} h(x)
  \qquad(d\ge0).
\]
The harmonic backward-mass predictor replaces these unknown exact weights
by an explicit multiple of $1/x$; this replacement is \emph{conjectural}, not
part of the conservation theorem. The new theoretical connection is
Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}, which proves the \emph{lower} half of the proposed
Syracuse residue-class profile of $a\,h(a)$. The matching upper bound
\eqref{eq:upper}, and hence the predicted profile and absolute basin-density
formula, remain open (Corollary~\ref{cor:remains},
Conjectures~\ref{conj:profile} and~\ref{conj:formula}). The measurements in
Tables~\ref{table:syracuse} and~\ref{table:convergence} provide independent
numerical tests of these conjectures, but do not establish their limits.


\subsection*{Machine-checked finite core and its scope}
A companion Lean~4 development formalises the accelerated integer-valued
$3x+c$ map, its immediate predecessors for odd $c$, the recursion for
hitting a target within a fixed number of steps, and a finite weighted
hitting-mass identity for arbitrary finite samples. The separately checked
modules \texttt{Clipping.lean} and \texttt{ReturnPairs.lean} certify
finite truncation inequalities and the chronological return-pair identity. The links in the last row of Table~\ref{table:contributions} point to the
individual declarations, among them the finite return certificates and explicit
congruence witnesses. These statements are checked by the
\href{https://github.com/Sultanow/collatz/actions/workflows/collatz-lean.yml}{GitHub Actions workflow}
with Lean~4 and Mathlib. It builds every module, so that every declaration
passes the Lean kernel; it rejects \texttt{sorry}, \texttt{admit},
\texttt{native\_decide} and \texttt{axiom} declarations; and it fails unless
each theorem listed in \texttt{AxiomAudit.lean} depends at most on the axioms
\texttt{propext}, \texttt{Quot.sound} and \texttt{Classical.choice}. An
optional job replays the build in an independent kernel (\texttt{lake check}).
The links point to commit \href{https://github.com/Sultanow/collatz/commit/ee5a5b4}{\texttt{ee5a5b4}}, for which both jobs of the workflow, including the independent replay, ran successfully.

This formal verification is deliberately \emph{limited to finite dynamics}.
In particular, it does not certify Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens},
Theorem~\ref{thm:natural},
Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}'s logarithmic-density assertions,
Lemma~\ref{lem:model}, Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}, or the first-passage calibration and
tightness (Propositions~\ref{prop:clipped-calibration} and~\ref{prop:tightness}). Their proofs and external dependencies require separate
mathematical and, potentially, formal verification.

\section{Local structure: one forest, several trees}
\label{sec:local}
%=====================================================================
The local view grows the tree of all predecessors of a cycle. For the Collatz map this tree is usually restricted to the \emph{branching numbers}, the even numbers with an odd predecessor, and drawn as a Cayley tree with \emph{Leftward} edges through an odd predecessor and \emph{Upward} edges by doubling \cite{Sultanow_graph_2020}. The congruence-class bookkeeping of these trees for general $an+b$ maps, including their \emph{cotree densities}, is developed in detail by Kleinnijenhuis et al.\ \cite{Kleinnijenhuis_2026}. In this section we record two facts that hold for every map of the family alike.

Let $c$ be coprime to $6$. A \emph{branching number} is an even $n\in\N$ such that $(n-c)/3$ is an odd integer not divisible by $3$; let $S_c$ be the set of branching numbers. For $m\in S_c$ define the \emph{Leftward child} $L(m)=2^p\,(m-c)/3$ and the \emph{Upward child} $U(m)=2^r m$, where $p,r\ge1$ are minimal with $L(m),U(m)\in S_c$. Both exist because $2$ is a primitive root modulo $9$. Every branching number has exactly one parent, either as a Leftward or as an Upward child: the maps $L,U:S_c\to S_c$ are injective, and $S_c$ is the disjoint union of $L(S_c)$ and $U(S_c)$ \cite{Sultanow_graph_2020,Kleinnijenhuis_2026}. The parent of $n$ is found by halving $n$ until the result is either a branching number $m$, the Upward parent, or an odd number $o$, in which case $3o+c$ is the Leftward parent.

For $c=\pm1$, this halving stops after at most four steps, so whether $n$ is a Leftward or an Upward child depends only on $n\bmod 2^4\cdot18=288$. For $3x+1$, Kleinnijenhuis et al.\ count $27$ Leftward and $5$ Upward classes modulo $288$ \cite[(3.73), (3.79)]{Kleinnijenhuis_2026}, which gives the cotree density
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forest_density}
d\bigl(L(S_c)\bigr)+d\bigl(U(S_c)\bigr)=\frac{27}{288}+\frac{5}{288}=\frac{2}{18}=d(S_c).
\end{equation}
The same counts hold for $3x-1$, whose Upward classes are the negatives of those of $3x+1$ modulo $288$ (Table~\ref{table:mirror}). The identity~\eqref{eq:forest_density} gives the density of the whole forest $S_c$, not of the tree of the trivial cycle: for $3x-1$ that tree contains only about a third of all integers (Section~\ref{sec:basins}).

\subsection*{Local indistinguishability}
Let $\iota(n)=-n$. Then $T_{3,-1}=\iota\circ T_{3,1}\circ\iota$ on $\Z$. We call a set $A\subseteq\N$ \emph{locally defined} for a map $T$ if, for some fixed $m$ and modulus $M$, membership of $n$ in $A$ depends only on the residues of $n,T(n),\ldots,T^m(n)$ modulo $M$. The branching numbers, the Leftward and Upward children and the cotree classes are locally defined, and so is, for every word $B$ of length $m$, the set of $n$ whose first $m$ parities form $B$.

\begin{proposition}[Local indistinguishability]
\label{prop:local}
Let $A\subseteq\N$ be locally defined for $T_{3,1}$ by residue conditions $R$, and let $A^-\subseteq\N$ be the set defined for $T_{3,-1}$ by the negated conditions $-R$. Then $A$ and $A^-$ are periodic sets with the same natural density.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $T=T_{3,\pm1}$, the residue of $T(n)$ modulo $M$ depends only on $n$ modulo $2M$, hence the residue of $T^i(n)$ modulo $M$ depends only on $n$ modulo $2^iM$. Thus $A=\{n\in\N: n\bmod 2^mM\in Q\}$ for a set $Q$ of residues, and the same condition defines a periodic set $\tilde A\subseteq\Z$ for $T_{3,1}$ on all of $\Z$. Since $T_{3,-1}(n)=-T_{3,1}(-n)$, we have $n\in A^-$ if and only if $-n\in\tilde A$, that is, $A^-=\{n\in\N:-n\bmod 2^mM\in Q\}$. Both sets have density $|Q|/(2^mM)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:local}
A statement about $T_{3,1}$ that is formulated only in terms of the densities of locally defined sets holds if and only if the corresponding statement holds for $T_{3,-1}$. In particular, local branching statistics determine neither the number of cycles nor basin membership: $3x+1$ has one known cycle and $3x-1$ has three (Section~\ref{sec:zoo}), and Table~\ref{table:classes} shows that membership in a basin is not a function of any residue class.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Scope]
Corollary~\ref{cor:local} limits proof strategies that use only local branching statistics, such as cotree densities. It does not exclude proofs that combine the predecessor tree with global information that differs between $3x+1$ and $3x-1$: the size of the numbers involved, as in the descent $(x+1)/2<x$ for the map $x+1$, or the arithmetic of $2^l-3^k$ (Section~\ref{sec:words}).
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Residue classes do not decide membership}
Which tree of the forest a branching number belongs to is not a property of its residue class, for none of the moduli $18$, $288$ and $4608$ used in this kind of bookkeeping. Table~\ref{table:classes} shows this for $3x-1$, where all trees are known: members of the same class lie in different trees, in both directions. A check of the representatives $\ell=0$ can therefore refute a conjecture (the number $14$ refutes it for $3x-1$), but it cannot confirm one. For $3x+1$, the representatives $4$ and $16$ lie in the tree of the root; whether all members of $18\ell+4$ and $18\ell+16$ do is the Collatz conjecture itself. Section~\ref{sec:basins} shows more: for $3x-1$, $3x+5$ and $3x+13$, residue classes modulo powers of $2$ and $3$ do not even shift the basin shares measurably.

\section{Parity words and cycles}
\label{sec:words}
%=====================================================================
Fix odd $q\ge1$ and odd $c$, and write $T=T_{q,c}$. A \emph{word} $B=b_0b_1\cdots b_{l-1}\in\{0,1\}^l$ prescribes the parities of $l$ consecutive iterates. Its \emph{type} is $(l,k)$ with $k=b_0+\cdots+b_{l-1}$, the number of odd steps; we write
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:D}
D=D(l,k)=2^l-q^k .
\end{equation}
Let $0\le p_1<p_2<\cdots<p_k\le l-1$ be the positions of the ones in $B$; the position $0$ refers to the first step. The rotating-binary function of Cox et al.\ \cite{Cox_2021}, adapted from Halbeisen and Hungerbühler \cite{Halbeisen_Hungerbuehler_1997}, is
\begin{equation}
z_q(B) = \sum_{j=1}^{k}q^{\,k-j}\,2^{p_j}.
\label{eq:z}
\end{equation}
Let $\sigma B=b_1\cdots b_{l-1}b_0$ denote the left rotation of $B$, and let $\Zt$ be the ring of rationals with odd denominator. The following correspondence is classical \cite{Boehm_Sontacchi_1978,Lagarias_1990}; we state it in the notation of rotating binaries.

\begin{lemma}[Cycles from words; B\"ohm--Sontacchi, Lagarias]
\label{lem:words}
Let $B$ be a word of type $(l,k)$ with $D\neq0$, and put
\[
x_B=\frac{c\,z_q(B)}{D}\in\Zt .
\]
Then $x_B$ is a periodic point of $T$ (extended to $\Zt$) whose parity sequence is $BBB\cdots$, and it is the only such point in $\Zt$. The orbit of $x_B$ is $x_B, x_{\sigma B}, x_{\sigma^2B},\ldots$. Consequently:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item $T$ has an integer cycle with parity word $B$ if and only if $D\mid c\,z_q(B)$;
	\item for $k\ge1$ this cycle consists of positive integers if and only if $c\,D>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

It rests on the identity $T_B(x)=(q^kx+c\,z_q(B))/2^l$ for the composition $T_B$ of the branches $x/2$ and $(qx+c)/2$ along $B$, whose unique fixed point is $x_B$.

Every positive integer cycle of $T$ arises in this way from the word of its parities, and its members are obtained by rotating the word. This is the precise sense in which cycles are \emph{rotating binaries}. The reverse direction, asking for which maps a given word yields a cycle, is also classical: it is Lagarias' theory of rational cycles \cite{Lagarias_1990} and the shift--member duality of Belaga and Mignotte \cite[Theorem~4]{Belaga_Mignotte_DMTCS_2006}.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:971}
Take $q=5$ and the word $B=111110000000$ of type $(12,5)$. Then $D=2^{12}-5^5=971$. With $c=971$ we get $x_B=z_5(B)=5^4+5^3\cdot2+5^2\cdot2^2+5\cdot2^3+2^4=1031$, and the twelve rotations of $B$ produce the cycle
\[
(1031,3063,8143,20843,52593,131968,65984,32992,16496,8248,4124,2062)
\]
of $5x+971$. Its odd members are $1031,3063,8143,20843,52593$. The binary representation of $B$ reflects the course of the cycle: the ones mark the odd members.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Words with $D=c$.} If $c=2^l-q^k>0$, then $D=c$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:words} gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:D_equals_c}
x_B=z_q(B)\qquad\text{for every word $B$ of type $(l,k)$.}
\end{equation}
Every such word therefore yields a positive integer cycle of $T_{q,c}$, with members $z_q(\sigma^iB)$, and each necklace (rotation class) of type $(l,k)$ gives one cycle. This explains the cycles of Table~\ref{table:non_q1_cycles}, where $7=2^5-5^2$, $103=2^7-5^2$, $3=2^7-5^3$ and $131=2^8-5^3$, and the cycle families of $3x+5$ ($5=2^5-3^3$) and $3x+13$ ($13=2^8-3^5$) in Section~\ref{sec:zoo}.

\paragraph{Mirror and $D=1$.} The conjugation $x\mapsto-x$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mirror}
T_{q,-c}(x)=-T_{q,c}(-x),
\end{equation}
so the cycles of $3x-1$ are the negatives of the cycles of $3x+1$ on the negative integers. By Lemma~\ref{lem:words}(b) they come from words with $D<0$: the three cycles of $3x-1$ through $1$, $5$ and $17$ have the words $1$, $110$ and $11110111000$, with $D=-1$, $-1$ and $-139$ (since $2^3<3^2$ and $2^{11}<3^7$). If $q=2^m-1$, the word $10^{m-1}$ has $D=2^m-q=1$ and $z_q=1$, so $x_B=c$; for $c=1$ the number $1$ lies on a cycle. This is the power-of-two root $c=q+1=2^m$ of the family $a=2^m-1$ studied in \cite{Kleinnijenhuis_2026}.

Two further elementary remarks, on the case $q=1$ and on a product identity for cycles of the same type, are collected in Appendix~\ref{appx:cycles}.

\subsection*{Near-coincidences}
In every row of Table~\ref{table:zoo} with a non-trivial cycle, $|D|$ is small compared with $2^l$. For example, $2^{15}-181^2=32768-32761=7$, $2^7-5^3=128-125=3$ and $|D|/2^l=0.038$ for the type $(27,17)$ of $3x+5$. Equivalently, $l/k$ is a good rational approximation of $\log_2 q$:
\[
\begin{array}{rcll}
15/2 &=& 7.5 & \approx \log_2 181 = 7.49985,\\
7/3 &=& 2.333 & \approx \log_2 5 = 2.32193,\\
27/17,\ 8/5,\ 11/7 &=& 1.588,\ 1.600,\ 1.571 & \approx \log_2 3 = 1.58496 .
\end{array}
\]
For $3x-1$ the approximations come from below ($2^l<3^k$), in accordance with~\eqref{eq:mirror}. The heuristic count $E$ explains the pattern. A small $|D|$ makes the divisibility $D\mid c\,z_q(B)$ likely, while a large $|D|$ needs an astronomical number of words.

\subsection*{The near miss of \texorpdfstring{$3x+1$}{3x+1}}
The type $(8,5)$ illustrates where the $3x+1$ problem is decided.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:near_miss}
The $3x+1$ map has no cycle of type $(8,5)$, and the seven cycles of $3x+13$ of this type are exactly the seven candidates.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:words}, a $3x+1$ cycle of type $(8,5)$ requires $13=2^8-3^5$ to divide $z_3(B)$ for a word $B$ of that type. For $c=13$ we have $D=c$, so by~\eqref{eq:D_equals_c} the values $z_3(B)$ are exactly the members of the seven cycles of $3x+13$ of type $(8,5)$. Their minima $211,227,251,259,283,287,319$ are congruent to $3,6,4,12,10,1,7$ modulo $13$. Since $T_{3,13}$ maps non-multiples of $13$ to non-multiples of $13$, no member of these cycles is divisible by $13$.
\end{proof}

The heuristic expectation for this type is $E=7/13\approx0.54$; the actual count is $0$. Belaga and Mignotte call $2^l-3^k$ a Collatz number and the values $z_3(B)$ of the words of type $(l,k)$ that begin with an odd step its corona. In their language, Corollary~\ref{cor:near_miss} states that the Collatz number $2^8-3^5=13$ divides no member of its corona; they showed that the absence of non-trivial positive $3x+1$ cycles is equivalent to this property for every non-trivial type \cite[Theorem~5]{Belaga_Mignotte_DMTCS_2006}. For longer types, the methods of Steiner, Simons--de Weger and Hercher \cite{Steiner_1977,Simons_deWeger_2005,Hercher_2023} combine lower bounds for $|l\log2-k\log3|$ from Baker's theory with the verification limit. These bounds show that $D$ is too large for the divisibility to occur in cycles with few runs. Whatever proves the absence of cycles must see this arithmetic. The local structure of Section~\ref{sec:local} does not see it, because it is identical for $3x+1$ and $3x+13$ up to scaling.

\subsection*{All maps at once: rational cycles}
We now take the reverse perspective: instead of asking which cycles a given map has, we ask for which maps a given parity word yields a cycle. This is the point of view of Lagarias' rational cycles \cite{Lagarias_1990} and of the shift--member duality of Belaga and Mignotte \cite[Section~7]{Belaga_Mignotte_DMTCS_2006}. Lemma~\ref{lem:words} answers the question for every word. Let $d_0$ be the denominator of $z_3(B)/D$ in lowest terms. Then $x_B=c\,z_3(B)/D$ is a positive integer if and only if $c=\operatorname{sign}(D)\,m\,d_0$ with $m$ odd, and the cycle is $m$ times the \emph{primitive} cycle of $3x+c_0$, $c_0=\operatorname{sign}(D)\,d_0$. These are Lagarias' rational cycles of $3x+1$ with denominator $d_0$ \cite{Lagarias_1990}. A single pass over all words therefore lists the primitive cycles of all maps with $|c_0|\le C$. The denominator comes from the continued-fraction expansion of $z_3(B)/|D|$, which can be stopped as soon as a convergent has a denominator larger than $C$.

\begin{proposition}[Rational cycles]
\label{prop:rational}
For word length $l\le40$ there are exactly $1097$ primitive cycles of the maps $3x+c_0$ with $|c_0|\le1000$: $763$ with $c_0>0$ and $334$ with $c_0<0$, for $199$ distinct values of $|c_0|$. Every cycle of a map $3x+c$ with $|c|\le1000$ and word length at most $40$ is an odd multiple of one of them.
\end{proposition}

The computation checked all $2\,199\,023\,255\,510$ words in $512$ shards of about six minutes each on the HPC cluster of the EDIH Hamburg, with a parity check of every reported cycle. The program was validated against an independent implementation in exact rational arithmetic (for $l\le16$ and $|c_0|\le50$) and against the single-map census for $3x\pm1$, $3x+5$ and $3x+13$.

\paragraph{Comparison with the published tables.} For each of the $333$ values $0<k<1000$ with $\gcd(k,6)=1$, the primitive cycles of $3x+k$ listed by Belaga, Mignotte and Morley \cite{Belaga_Mignotte_2006,OEIS_A226607} that have at most $40$ elements coincide exactly with those of Proposition~\ref{prop:rational}; there are $763$ of them. The other $462$ listed cycles have between $41$ and $426$ elements, and for $153$ of these maps every known primitive cycle is longer than $40$. Proposition~\ref{prop:rational} therefore turns the conjecturally complete tables into a theorem for all cycles with at most $40$ elements and $k<1000$.

\paragraph{Heuristic.} If $z_3(B)$ were equidistributed modulo $|D|$, the reduced denominator of $z_3(B)/|D|$ would equal a divisor $e$ of $|D|$ with probability $\varphi(e)/|D|$. A primitive cycle corresponds to one aperiodic necklace of words, so the expected number of primitive cycles of type $(l,k)$ with $|c_0|\le C$ is
\[
E_C(l,k)=L(l,k)\,\frac{S_C(D)}{|D|},\qquad S_C(D)=\sum_{e\,\mid\,|D|,\ e\le C}\varphi(e),
\]
where $L(l,k)$ is the number of aperiodic binary necklaces of type $(l,k)$. If all divisors of $|D|$ are at most $C$, then $S_C(D)=|D|$, every necklace yields a cycle, and $E_C$ is exact; for example, $D(10,5)=781=11\cdot71$ gives all $25$ necklaces. Summed over $l\le40$, the heuristic predicts $1148.7$ primitive cycles against $1097$ observed, and it reproduces the counts per word length including the peaks (Figure~\ref{fig:cycles}). The agreement has a deterministic and a statistical part. For the $44$ types with $|D|\le1000$, all divisors of $|D|$ are admissible, every necklace yields a cycle, and the $227$ cycles of these types are predicted exactly. For the other $116$ types with $E_{1000}\ge1$, the heuristic predicts $879.8$ cycles and we observe $839$; the remaining types with $E_{1000}<1$ contribute $41.9$ expected and $31$ observed cycles. The deviations have a systematic and a statistical part (Run~J). The deficit comes from the types with $l$ and $k$ even and $D>0$: for these $46$ types the heuristic predicts $379.8$ cycles and we observe $329$, whereas the $49$ types with $\gcd(l,k)=1$ give $348$ cycles against $338.2$. For even $l$ and $k$, $D=(2^{l/2}-3^{k/2})(2^{l/2}+3^{k/2})$, and every periodic word $B=B'B'$ satisfies $P\mid z_3(B)$ for $P=2^{l/2}+3^{k/2}$, because $z_3(B'B')=P\,z_3(B')$. These words give no primitive cycles of type $(l,k)$, and the aperiodic words are not equidistributed modulo $P$: over the $38$ types with $l\le28$, $D>0$ and at least five expected multiples, only $0.61$ times the expected proportion $1/P$ of the aperiodic words have $P\mid z_3(B)$; for the $9$ such types with $D<0$ the factor is $1.40$. The heuristic thus fails for the algebraic factors of $2^l-3^k$. In addition, the counts scatter less than a Poisson model allows: over the $116$ types, $\sum(O-E)^2/E=71.4$, a dispersion index of $0.62$, and the index is $0.64$ for the coprime types alone. It depends on the bound $C$, however: an exact count of all words with $l\le30$ gives the index $0.55$ for $C=1000$ and $1.09$ for $C=2\cdot10^4$.

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:heuristic}

\section{Measuring forests: basin densities}
\label{sec:basins}
%=====================================================================
For a cycle $\mathcal C$ of $T_{q,c}$, its \emph{basin} is $\beta(\mathcal C)=\{n\in\N: T^j(n)\in\mathcal C \text{ for some } j\}$, and we write $d_N(\mathcal C)=|\beta(\mathcal C)\cap[1,N]|/N$. The basins are the trees of the forest of Section~\ref{sec:local}; the local structure fixes only the total density of all trees. We report empirical shares $d_N$. In general, the existence of the natural density $\lim_{N\to\infty}d_N$ is open; Propositions~\ref{prop:filtration} and~\ref{prop:cosets} establish densities for certain invariant classes, not automatically for individual basins. Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} below establishes the logarithmic density of every basin in the stated family; a natural-density result for $3x+1$ follows using a recent result of Mazur (Corollary~\ref{cor:natural}).

Two empirical entries of Table~\ref{table:basins}, $0.20000$ and $0.07692$, are close to $1/5$ and $1/13$, respectively. The following elementary gcd invariance explains the scaling underlying these values, but their exact equality to those fractions would additionally require that the $3x+1$ basin of $1$ have density one. The invariance is implicit in Lagarias' primitive cycles \cite{Lagarias_1990}, explicit in \cite[(19)]{Belaga_Mignotte_DMTCS_2006} and also used by Wegner \cite{Wegner_2021}.

\begin{proposition}[Divisibility filtration \cite{Lagarias_1990,Belaga_Mignotte_DMTCS_2006,Wegner_2021}]
\label{prop:filtration}
Let $\gcd(c,6)=1$ and, for a divisor $e$ of $|c|$, let $B_e=\{n\ge1:\gcd(n,c)=e\}$. Then $T_{3,c}(B_e)\subseteq B_e$, the map $n\mapsto n/e$ conjugates $T_{3,c}$ on $B_e$ to $T_{3,c/e}$ on the integers coprime to $c/e$, and $B_e$ has natural density $\varphi(|c|/e)/|c|$.
\end{proposition}

Indeed, every prime divisor of $c$ is at least $5$, so that $T_{3,c}$ preserves $\gcd(n,c)$, and $T_{3,c}(em)=e\,T_{3,c/e}(m)$ for odd $e$.

Consequently, for every cycle $\mathcal C$ of $3x+c/e$ coprime to $c/e$, the basin of $e\,\mathcal C$ in $3x+c$ is the dilation by $e$ of the basin of $\mathcal C$ in $3x+c/e$. Its logarithmic density is therefore exactly $1/e$ times the logarithmic density of the smaller basin; the same scaling holds for natural densities whenever they exist. Proposition~\ref{prop:filtration} determines the densities of the gcd classes $B_e$, whereas an individual basin may occupy only a portion of its class. If $B_1$ contains a single known cycle, that basin exhausts $B_1$ only if all other orbits in $B_1$ form a set of density zero (for the chosen notion of density). In particular, writing $\beta_c(a)$ for the basin of the cycle through $a$ under $T_{3,c}$, we have the unconditional logarithmic-density identities
\[
\delta(\beta_5(5))=\tfrac15\,\delta(\beta_1(1)),\qquad
\delta(\beta_{13}(13))=\tfrac1{13}\,\delta(\beta_1(1)).
\]
The empirical values $1/5$ and $1/13$ become exact individual-basin densities if $\delta(\beta_1(1))=1$; the corresponding claim for natural densities likewise requires natural-density one for that basin.

The divisibility filtration has a multiplicative refinement. For $c$ coprime to $6$, let $G=(\mathbb Z/c\mathbb Z)^\times$ and let $H$ be the subgroup of $G$ generated by $2$ and $3$.

\begin{proposition}[Multiplicative filtration]
\label{prop:cosets}
Let $c\ge1$ with $\gcd(c,6)=1$. For every coset $gH$, the set $B_{1,gH}=\{n\in\N:\gcd(n,c)=1,\ n\bmod c\in gH\}$ is completely invariant under $T_{3,c}$, and it has natural density $\varphi(c)/(c\,[G:H])$. In particular, if $p\equiv\pm1\pmod{24}$ is prime, the Legendre symbol $(n/p)$ is constant along every orbit of $T_{3,p}$ on the integers prime to $p$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $n$ prime to $c$, $T_{3,c}(n)$ is congruent to $2^{-1}n$ or to $3\cdot2^{-1}n$ modulo $c$, and both factors lie in $H$. Conversely, every preimage of an integer prime to $c$ is prime to $c$. Hence $B_{1,gH}$ is completely invariant. It is the union of $|H|$ residue classes modulo $c$, so its density is $|H|/c=\varphi(c)/(c\,[G:H])$. For a prime $p\equiv\pm1\pmod{24}$, both $2$ and $3$ are quadratic residues modulo $p$, so $H$ consists of quadratic residues.
\end{proof}

The proposition applies within every gcd class $B_e$ as well, with $c/e$ in place of $c$. The density $\varphi(c)/(c\,[G:H])$ belongs unconditionally to the invariant \emph{coset set}. Even if a coset contains only one known cycle, the basin of that cycle need not exhaust the coset: other cycles or nonconvergent orbits have not been excluded. Its basin has the full rational coset density only if the complement within the coset has density zero. The invariance is elementary and closely related to the Markov chains on residue classes of generalized Collatz maps studied by Matthews \cite{Matthews_2010}; we have not found it stated for the maps $3x+c$. The predicted coset shares are confirmed numerically below.

\begin{proposition}[Verification]
\label{prop:verification}
For $3x-1$ and the $17$ maps $3x+c$ with $0<c<50$ and $\gcd(c,6)=1$, every orbit of $1\le n\le10^{10}$ reaches one of the cycles of Proposition~\ref{prop:rational} or one of the three cycles with longer words found above.
\end{proposition}

The type of a cycle, its length and number of odd steps, therefore does not determine its attractiveness.

\subsection*{Existence of the densities}
For $A\subseteq\N$, the \emph{logarithmic density} is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:logdens}
\delta(A)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{\substack{n<N\\ n\in A}}\frac1n,
\end{equation}
if the limit exists. If the natural density of $A$ exists, then $\delta(A)$ exists and has the same value; the converse fails in general. Tao proved his theorem on almost bounded orbits in logarithmic density \cite{Tao_2022}, and Gon\c{c}alves, Greenfeld and Madrid extended his method to a class of maps that contains $T_{3,c}$ \cite{GGM_2025}. They observed numerically that the predecessor set of an integer $a$ appears to have a natural density $\kappa(a)$, and that each of the three cycles of $3x-1$ attracts about a third of all integers; they also proposed a formulation with logarithmically distributed starting values \cite[Remark~1.7]{GGM_2025}. In this logarithmic form, existence follows from their stabilisation of first passages, because a basin is not changed by first passage. For natural densities see Corollary~\ref{cor:natural} and Remark~\ref{rem:mazur}.

We call $A\subseteq\N$ \emph{completely invariant} under $T$ if $n\in A\iff T(n)\in A$. Every basin is completely invariant, and so is the set of starting values with unbounded orbits.

\begin{theorem}[Logarithmic basin densities]
\label{thm:logdens}
Let $c\ge-1$ be odd, and let $A\subseteq\N$ be completely invariant under $T_{3,c}$. Then $\delta(A)$ exists. In particular, the basin of every cycle $\mathcal C$ of $T_{3,c}$ has a logarithmic density $\delta(\mathcal C)$, and if $\lim_{N\to\infty}d_N(\mathcal C)$ exists, it equals $\delta(\mathcal C)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[The basin of $1$ for $3x+1$]
\label{cor:collatz}
The positive integers whose $3x+1$ orbit reaches $1$ form a set with a logarithmic density.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $c=1$ this set is the basin of the cycle $(1,2)$ of $T_{3,1}$, which is completely invariant.
\end{proof}

The Collatz conjecture asserts that this set is $\N$, so that its density is $1$. Corollary~\ref{cor:collatz} does not decide this, but it shows unconditionally that the logarithmic proportion of these integers up to $N$ converges. By Mazur \cite{Mazur_PD_2026} the density is positive, and by \cite{Mazur_Pred_2026} it equals $1$ if and only if the Collatz conjecture holds, because a single counterexample would have predecessors of positive lower density. Corollary~\ref{cor:natural} below upgrades the existence to natural density by Mazur's transport estimate; for $3x+1$, the existence of natural densities of predecessor sets is also stated in the formalisation \cite{CLT_2026}. For maps with several basins, such as $3x-1$ and $3x+5$, Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} gives the existence of the densities of all basins, whose values no conjecture predicts.

For the proof we use the Syracuse form of $T_{3,c}$ on the odd integers, $\operatorname{Syr}(m)=(3m+c)/2^{\nu_2(3m+c)}$, where $\nu_2$ is the $2$-adic valuation. For $x\ge1$ and odd $m$, the \emph{first passage time} below $x$ is $\tau_x(m)=\inf\{j\ge0:\operatorname{Syr}^j(m)\le x\}$, and the \emph{first passage} is $\operatorname{Pass}_x(m)=\operatorname{Syr}^{\tau_x(m)}(m)$, with $\operatorname{Pass}_x(m)=1$ if $\tau_x(m)=\infty$. Let $\mu(I)=\sum_{m\in I,\ m\text{ odd}}1/m$, so that $\mu([u,v))=\tfrac12\log(v/u)+O(1/u)$. For $1\le u<v$ let $\mathbf{Log}_{u,v}$ be a random odd integer in $[u,v)$ with $\mathbb P(\mathbf{Log}_{u,v}=m)=1/(m\,\mu([u,v)))$, and write $d_{\mathrm{TV}}$ for the total variation distance. The map $n\mapsto n/2$ ($n$ even), $n\mapsto 3n+c$ ($n$ odd) belongs to the class of \cite[Definition~1.2]{GGM_2025} with $p=2$, $q=3$ and $r(1)=c$, and its Syracuse map is $\operatorname{Syr}$; the condition $c\ge-1$ makes it map $\N$ into itself. It satisfies the hypotheses (a)--(c) of \cite[Theorem~1.3]{GGM_2025}: $\gcd(2,3)=1$, $3<2^{2}$, and $3+c$ is even. These hypotheses impose no condition on $\gcd(3,c)$. The proof of \cite[Proposition~3.5]{GGM_2025}, however, is carried out after the normalisation $\gcd(q,r(1))=1$ of \cite[Section~2.2]{GGM_2025}, that is, for $3\nmid c$; Step~0 below reduces Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} to this case.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens}]
\emph{Step 0 (factors $3$ in $c$).} Suppose $3\mid c$, and write $c=3c'$ with $c'\ge1$ odd. Then $T_{3,c}(3m)=3\,T_{3,c'}(m)$ for all $m$, and $T_{3,c}(n)=3(n+c')/2$ for odd $n$. Hence $A'=\{m:3m\in A\}$ is completely invariant under $T_{3,c'}$, and an odd $n$ lies in $A$ if and only if $(n+c')/2\in A'$. Since
\[
\sum_{\substack{n<Y,\ n\text{ odd}\\ (n+c')/2\in A'}}\frac1n=\frac12\sum_{\substack{u<Y/2\\ u\in A'}}\frac1u+O(1),
\]
Step~1 below shows that $\delta(A)$ exists and equals $\delta(A')$ whenever $\delta(A')$ exists. Repeating this step, we may assume $3\nmid c$.

\emph{Input.} For odd $c\ge-1$ with $3\nmid c$, the stabilisation of first passages of Gon\c{c}alves, Greenfeld and Madrid \cite[Proposition~3.5]{GGM_2025}, the analogue of \cite[Proposition~1.11]{Tao_2022}, provides $\varepsilon_0\in(0,1)$ such that for all $\alpha,\lambda\in(1,1+\varepsilon_0)$ and all $x\ge2$
\begin{align}
\mathbb P\bigl(\tau_x(\mathbf{Log}_{x^\lambda,x^{\alpha\lambda}})=\infty\bigr)&\ll x^{-\varepsilon_0},\label{eq:ggm_hit}\\
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\bigl(\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf{Log}_{x^\lambda,x^{\alpha\lambda}}),\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf{Log}_{x^{\alpha\lambda},x^{\alpha^2\lambda}})\bigr)&\ll(\log x)^{-\varepsilon_0},\label{eq:ggm_tv}
\end{align}
with implied constants that do not depend on $x$. Choose $\varepsilon_1>0$ with $(1+\varepsilon_1)^2<1+\varepsilon_0$ and fix $\lambda_0\in(1,1+\varepsilon_1)$.

\emph{Step 1 (odd numbers).} Write $n=2^km$ with $m$ odd. Since $T_{3,c}^k(n)=m$, we have $n\in A$ if and only if $m\in A$. The odd part $A_1=A\cap(2\N+1)$ satisfies $m\in A_1\iff\operatorname{Syr}(m)\in A_1$. Let $\kappa(Y)=\mu(A_1\cap[1,Y))/\mu([1,Y))$. If $\kappa(Y)\to\delta$, then
\[
\sum_{\substack{n<N\\ n\in A}}\frac1n=\sum_{k\ge0}2^{-k}\mu\bigl(A_1\cap[1,N/2^k)\bigr)=\delta\log N+o(\log N),
\]
so $\delta(A)=\delta$. It remains to show that $\kappa(Y)$ converges.

\emph{Step 2 (invariance under first passage).} If $m>x$ and $\tau_x(m)<\infty$, then $m\in A_1\iff\operatorname{Pass}_x(m)\in A_1$. Hence, for every random odd integer $\mathbf m>x$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:passage}
\bigl|\mathbb P(\mathbf m\in A_1)-\mathbb P\bigl(\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf m)\in A_1\bigr)\bigr|\le\mathbb P\bigl(\tau_x(\mathbf m)=\infty\bigr).
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 3 (adjacent windows).} Let $\alpha\in(1,1+\varepsilon_1)$ and $F_\alpha(y)=\mathbb P(\mathbf{Log}_{y,y^\alpha}\in A_1)=\mu(A_1\cap[y,y^\alpha))/\mu([y,y^\alpha))$. For $y\ge4$ put $x=y^{1/\lambda_0}$. The windows $[y,y^\alpha)=[x^{\lambda_0},x^{\alpha\lambda_0})$ and $[y^\alpha,y^{\alpha^2})=[x^{\alpha\lambda_0},x^{\alpha^2\lambda_0})$ lie above $x$, and $\alpha\lambda_0<1+\varepsilon_0$. By~\eqref{eq:passage} for both windows, \eqref{eq:ggm_hit} with $\lambda=\lambda_0$ and $\lambda=\alpha\lambda_0$, and~\eqref{eq:ggm_tv} with $\lambda=\lambda_0$,
\[
|F_\alpha(y)-F_\alpha(y^\alpha)|\ll x^{-\varepsilon_0}+(\log x)^{-\varepsilon_0}\ll(\log y)^{-\varepsilon_0}.
\]

\emph{Step 4 (a limit that is periodic in $\log\log y$).} Put $t=\log\log y$, $a=\log\alpha$ and $f_\alpha(t)=F_\alpha(\exp e^t)$. Then $f_\alpha(t+a)=F_\alpha(y^\alpha)$, so $|f_\alpha(t)-f_\alpha(t+a)|\ll e^{-\varepsilon_0t}$. These differences are summable along $t,t+a,t+2a,\ldots$, so $\psi_\alpha(t)=\lim_{k\to\infty}f_\alpha(t+ka)$ exists, $\psi_\alpha(t+a)=\psi_\alpha(t)$, and $|f_\alpha(t)-\psi_\alpha(t)|\ll e^{-\varepsilon_0t}$. For $W=[y,y^\alpha)$, $W'=[y^{1+\eta},y^{\alpha(1+\eta)})$ and $0<\eta\le1$,
\[
|F_\alpha(y)-F_\alpha(y^{1+\eta})|\le\frac{2\mu(W\triangle W')}{\mu(W)}\le\frac{2(1+\alpha)\eta}{\alpha-1}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log y}\Bigr).
\]
Since the shift in $t$ is $\log(1+\eta)$, this gives $|f_\alpha(t)-f_\alpha(t')|\ll_\alpha|t-t'|+e^{-t}$ for $t\le t'\le t+\log2$, so $\psi_\alpha$ is Lipschitz continuous.

\emph{Step 5 (from windows to $\kappa$).} Let $\tilde\kappa(t)=\kappa(\exp e^t)$ and $h(t)=\mu([1,\exp e^t))=\tfrac12e^t+O(1)$. The identity $\mu(A_1\cap[y,y^\alpha))=F_\alpha(y)\,\mu([y,y^\alpha))$ reads $\tilde\kappa(t+a)h(t+a)=\tilde\kappa(t)h(t)+f_\alpha(t)\bigl(h(t+a)-h(t)\bigr)$, hence
\[
\tilde\kappa(t+a)=e^{-a}\tilde\kappa(t)+(1-e^{-a})f_\alpha(t)+O(e^{-t}).
\]
For fixed $t$, the numbers $D_k=\tilde\kappa(t+ka)-\psi_\alpha(t)$ satisfy $|D_{k+1}|\le e^{-a}|D_k|+O(e^{-\varepsilon_0(t+ka)})$, because $\psi_\alpha(t+ka)=\psi_\alpha(t)$ and $\varepsilon_0<1$. Thus $D_k\to0$ uniformly for $t$ in compact intervals, and $\tilde\kappa(t)-\psi_\alpha(t)\to0$ as $t\to\infty$.

\emph{Step 6 (two incommensurable windows).} Let $\alpha'=\alpha^{1/\sqrt2}$ and $a'=\log\alpha'$. Steps 3--5 for $\alpha'$ give a continuous $a'$-periodic function $\psi_{\alpha'}$ with $\tilde\kappa(t)-\psi_{\alpha'}(t)\to0$, so $\psi_\alpha(t)-\psi_{\alpha'}(t)\to0$. Fix $t$. As $\psi_\alpha(t+ka)=\psi_\alpha(t)$, we get $\psi_{\alpha'}(t+ka)\to\psi_\alpha(t)$ for $k\to\infty$. Since $a/a'=\sqrt2$ is irrational, the points $t+ka$ with $k\ge k_0$ are dense modulo $a'$ for every $k_0$, and $\psi_{\alpha'}$ is continuous; hence $\psi_{\alpha'}$ is constant, equal to $\psi_\alpha(t)$. As $t$ was arbitrary, $\psi_\alpha$ and $\psi_{\alpha'}$ are the same constant $\delta$, and $\kappa(Y)\to\delta$ by Step~5.

Finally, if the natural density of $A$ exists, partial summation shows that the logarithmic density exists with the same value.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Consequences and limits]
\label{rem:consequences}
The set of starting values with unbounded orbits is completely invariant as well, so it also has a logarithmic density. Existence is shared by all maps of the family, in particular by $3x+1$ and $3x-1$; what separates them are the values $\delta(\mathcal C)$. The stabilisation of first passages controls logarithmic averages only; natural densities need the additional transport of Theorem~\ref{thm:natural}. Maps $3x+c$ with $c\le-3$ do not map $\N$ into itself and are not covered; by~\eqref{eq:mirror} they are the maps $3x+|c|$ on the negative integers.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[A first-passage formula]
\label{rem:passage}
For $3\nmid c$, Steps~2, 4 and~6 and~\eqref{eq:ggm_hit} give, for all $\alpha,\lambda\in(1,1+\varepsilon_1)$,
\[
\delta(\mathcal C)=\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathbb P\bigl(\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf{Log}_{x^{\lambda},x^{\alpha\lambda}})\in\beta(\mathcal C)\bigr).
\]
The density of a basin is the probability that a logarithmically random starting value far above $x$ first enters $[1,x]$ inside that basin. In the proofs of \cite{Tao_2022,GGM_2025}, the law of $\operatorname{Pass}_x$ is controlled through the distribution of the Syracuse offsets modulo powers of $3$. Problem~\ref{prob:basins}(c) thus becomes a question about this $3$-adic law on the small members of each basin.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} does not reach natural counting. For $3x+1$, Mazur \cite{Mazur_2026} has compared the first passage of the uniform law with that of the logarithmic law on the same block. His preprint is not yet refereed, but its proof chain is formalised in Lean~4; the following corollary relies on it.

\begin{corollary}[Natural densities for $3x+1$]
\label{cor:natural}
Every set $A\subseteq\N$ that is completely invariant under $T_{3,1}$ has a natural density, and it equals $\delta(A)$. In particular, the positive integers whose $3x+1$ orbit reaches $1$ form a set with a natural density.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $\alpha=1001/1000$ as in \cite{Mazur_2026}. For large $N$ put $x=N^{1/\alpha^2}$ and $y=x^\alpha$; the odd integers in $[y,y^\alpha]=[N^{1/\alpha},N]$ form a block of \cite[Theorem~3.1]{Mazur_2026}. Let $\mathbf U_y$ be uniform and $\mathbf L_y$ logarithmic on this block. By~\eqref{eq:passage}, by the scheduled no-hit bound and the $\ell^1$ transport bound of \cite[Theorem~3.1]{Mazur_2026}, and by the no-hit bound \cite[(1.18)]{Tao_2022} for $\mathbf L_y$, which \cite{Mazur_2026} uses with this $\alpha$,
\[
\mathbb P(\mathbf U_y\in A_1)=\mathbb P(\mathbf L_y\in A_1)+o(1)\qquad(N\to\infty).
\]
Steps~4 and~6 give $\mathbb P(\mathbf L_y\in A_1)\to\delta(A)$: the window $[y,y^\alpha)$ splits into $K$ consecutive windows of ratio $\alpha^{1/K}<1+\varepsilon_1$, and $\mathbb P(\mathbf L_y\in A_1)$ is their $\mu$-weighted average; the endpoint conventions differ by at most one integer. The odd integers below $N^{1/\alpha}$ form a proportion $O(N^{1/\alpha-1})$ of the odd integers below $N$. Hence $A_1$ has natural density $\delta(A)$ relative to the odd integers. Since $2^km\in A$ if and only if $m\in A$, each set $\{2^km:m\text{ odd}\}$ meets $A$ in the same relative density, and $A$ has natural density $\delta(A)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Two routes to natural density]
\label{rem:mazur}
Mazur's argument combines Tao's first-passage architecture with a discrepancy bound for the rotation by $\log_2 3$, which he derives from Rhin's irrationality measure \cite{Mazur_2026}; its proof chain is formalised in Lean for $3x+1$. Allikvere \cite{Allikvere_2026} conditions Tao's Fourier-decay and fine-scale-mixing estimates on the total $2$-valuation and uses an effective irrationality measure for $\log_23$ at a single point. Both arguments see the map only through the Syracuse offset. For $T_{3,c}$ this offset is $c$ times that of $3x+1$: in the notation of \cite{Tao_2022}, $\operatorname{Syr}^n(m)=3^n2^{-|\vec a|}m+c\,F_n(\vec a)$. For $3\nmid c$ the Syracuse random variable is therefore replaced by $c\cdot\mathbf{Syrac}(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)$, whose law is the image of the law of $\mathbf{Syrac}(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)$ under multiplication by a unit. Appendix~\ref{appx:natural} carries out the transfer for Allikvere's argument.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Natural densities for all maps $3x+c$]
\label{thm:natural}
Assume that the uniform-measure first-passage analysis of \cite[Section~6]{Allikvere_2026} is correct. Let $c\ge-1$ be odd. Then every set $A\subseteq\N$ that is completely invariant under $T_{3,c}$ has a natural density, and it equals $\delta(A)$.
\end{theorem}

The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{appx:natural}. The theorem rests on a preprint that is not yet refereed; for $c=1$, Corollary~\ref{cor:natural} gives an independent route through \cite{Mazur_2026}. Nontrivial bounds for the values of the densities remain open.

\subsection*{The predecessor potential}
The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} applies to predecessor sets as well, and their densities satisfy exact identities along the backward trees. For $a\in\N$ let
\[
P(a)=\{n\in\N:\ T_{3,c}^k(n)=a\text{ for some }k\ge0\}.
\]
For a cycle $\mathcal C$, let $L_0(\mathcal C)$ be the set of the predecessors of its members that do not lie on $\mathcal C$, and let $L_{d+1}(\mathcal C)=T_{3,c}^{-1}(L_d(\mathcal C))$. These are the levels of the backward tree of $\mathcal C$.

\begin{proposition}[Predecessor potential]
\label{prop:potential}
Let $c\ge-1$ be odd with $3\nmid c$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Every predecessor set $P(a)$, $a\in\N$, has a logarithmic density $h(a)$.
	\item If $a$ is not periodic, then $h(a)=h(2a)+h\bigl((2a-c)/3\bigr)$, where the last term is omitted unless $(2a-c)/3$ is a positive integer. If $3\mid a$, then $h(a)=0$.
	\item For every cycle $\mathcal C$ and every $a\in\mathcal C$, $h(a)=\delta(\mathcal C)$, and for every level $d\ge0$
	\[
	\delta(\mathcal C)=\sum_{x\in L_d(\mathcal C)}h(x).
	\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) Write $a=2^ja'$ with $a'$ odd. We follow the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} with $A=P(a)$ and use one more consequence of the proof of \cite[Proposition~3.5]{GGM_2025}: after decreasing $\varepsilon_0$ if necessary, for all $\alpha,\lambda\in(1,1+\varepsilon_0)$ and $x\ge2$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ggm_low}
\mathbb P\bigl(\tau_x(\mathbf L)<\infty,\ \operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf L)\le\sqrt x\bigr)\ll(\log x)^{-\varepsilon_0},\qquad\mathbf L=\mathbf{Log}_{x^\lambda,x^{\alpha\lambda}}.
\end{equation}
To justify this additional estimate, we extract a consequence of the \emph{proof} of \cite[Proposition~3.5, especially (4.7)--(4.9)]{GGM_2025}; it is not stated there as a separate result. For the logarithmically distributed $\mathbf L$ in the indicated windows, (4.7) bounds the complement of the regular event $a^{(n_0)}(\mathbf L)\in\mathcal A^{n_0}$ of \cite{GGM_2025}, on which the partial sums of the first $n_0$ valuations are typical, by $O((\log x)^{-\gamma})$ for some $\gamma>0$. On that event, the proof of Proposition~3.5 shows that $1\le\tau_x(\mathbf L)\le n_0$, and (4.9) yields, for $0\le m,n\le n_0$,
\[
 \operatorname{Syr}^n(\mathbf L)\ge \exp\!\bigl(- (\log x)^{0.7}\bigr)
 \left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{n-m}\operatorname{Syr}^m(\mathbf L).
\]
Taking $n=\tau_x(\mathbf L)$ and $m=n-1$ (which are within this range) and using $\operatorname{Syr}^{n-1}(\mathbf L)>x$ gives
\[
 \operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf L)>
 \frac34 x\exp\!\bigl(-(\log x)^{0.7}\bigr)>\sqrt{x}
\]
for all sufficiently large $x$. Thus the event in~\eqref{eq:ggm_low} is contained in the exceptional event of (4.7) for sufficiently large $x$. Decreasing $\varepsilon_0$ so that $\varepsilon_0\le\gamma$ proves~\eqref{eq:ggm_low}; bounded $x$ are absorbed into the implied constant. The same argument gives the sharper bound
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ggm_low2}
\mathbb P\Bigl(\tau_x(\mathbf L)<\infty,\ \operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf L)\le\tfrac34x\exp\bigl(-(\log x)^{0.7}\bigr)\Bigr)\ll(\log x)^{-\varepsilon_0},
\end{equation}
which is used in Appendix~\ref{appx:lower}.

\emph{Step 1.} Let $n=2^km$ with $m$ odd. If $n$ is not of the form $2^ia$, then $a$ is not among $n,n/2,\dots,2m$, so $n\in P(a)$ if and only if $m\in P(a)$. The numbers $2^ia$ have a finite sum of reciprocals. Hence it suffices to show that $\kappa(Y)$ converges for $A_1=P(a)\cap(2\N+1)$.

\emph{Step 2.} Let $x\ge a$, let $m>x$ be odd with $\tau_x(m)<\infty$, and put $M=\operatorname{Pass}_x(m)$. Before $M$, the orbit of $m$ under $T_{3,c}$ consists of Syracuse iterates, which exceed $x$, and of numbers $2^im'$ with $i\ge1$, where $m'$ is a Syracuse iterate up to $M$. Such a number equals $a$ only if $m'=a'$, and then $m'=M$, because the earlier iterates exceed $x\ge a'$. Hence, if $M\ne a'$, the orbit of $m$ meets $a$ at $M$ or later, if at all, so that $m\in P(a)\iff M\in P(a)$. For every random odd integer $\mathbf m>x$ this gives
\[
\bigl|\mathbb P(\mathbf m\in A_1)-\mathbb P\bigl(\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf m)\in A_1\bigr)\bigr|\le\mathbb P\bigl(\tau_x(\mathbf m)=\infty\bigr)+\mathbb P\bigl(\tau_x(\mathbf m)<\infty,\ \operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf m)=a'\bigr).
\]
For the two windows of Step~3 and $x\ge a'^2$, the last probability is $\ll(\log x)^{-\varepsilon_0}$ by~\eqref{eq:ggm_low}. This is the size of the error in Step~3, so Steps~3--6 apply without change.

(b) Let $a$ be not periodic. Then an orbit meets $a$ at most once, and it does so from exactly one predecessor of $a$. The predecessors of $a$ are $2a$ and, if it is a positive integer, the odd number $(2a-c)/3$. Hence $P(a)$ is the disjoint union of $\{a\}$, $P(2a)$ and $P((2a-c)/3)$, and the identity follows from~(a), because the logarithmic density is finitely additive. If $3\mid a$, then $3\nmid2a-c$, so $2a$ is the only predecessor of $a$, and it is divisible by $3$. Hence $P(a)=\{2^ia:i\ge0\}$, and $h(a)=0$.

(c) For $a\in\mathcal C$ we have $P(a)=\beta(\mathcal C)$. Every $n\in\beta(\mathcal C)\setminus\mathcal C$ has a last iterate outside $\mathcal C$, which lies in $L_0(\mathcal C)$. Hence $\beta(\mathcal C)$ is the disjoint union of $\mathcal C$ and the sets $P(x)$, $x\in L_0(\mathcal C)$, and $L_0(\mathcal C)$ is finite, which gives the identity for $d=0$. No member of a level is periodic, so (b) applies to each of them, and the sum over $L_{d+1}(\mathcal C)$ equals the sum over $L_d(\mathcal C)$.
\end{proof}

We call $h$ the \emph{predecessor potential} of the map. Part~(a) answers the existence question of \cite[Remark~1.7]{GGM_2025} for every $a$, in logarithmic form; the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:natural} applies to predecessor sets as well (Appendix~\ref{appx:natural}), so that $h(a)$ is also a natural density, conditionally on \cite{Allikvere_2026} (for $c=1$ see also \cite{CLT_2026}). Part~(c) is a conservation law: the density of a basin is distributed over every level of the backward tree of its cycle. For $3x+1$, the existence of the predecessor densities as natural densities and their flow identity are stated in the formalisation \cite{CLT_2026}; Proposition~\ref{prop:potential} holds for the whole family, in logarithmic density. For $3x+1$ and $3x-1$, recent positivity results complete the picture.

\begin{corollary}[No sparse invariant sets for $3x\pm1$]
\label{cor:dichotomy}
Let $c\in\{1,-1\}$. Then $h(a)>0$ if and only if $3\nmid a$, and every nonempty completely invariant set has positive logarithmic density. In particular, the three basins of $3x-1$ of the cycles through $1$, $5$ and $17$ have positive logarithmic densities, and these add up to $1$ if and only if every orbit of $3x-1$ enters one of the three cycles.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $3\nmid a$. We make the sign restriction in the application of \cite[Theorem~A]{ElJaouhari_2026} explicit. For $b\in\mathbb Z$ let
\[
 \pi_b(X)=\#\{z\in\mathbb Z:|z|\le X,\ T_{3,1}^j(z)=b\text{ for some }j\ge0\}.
\]
El Jaouhari proves that, when $3\nmid b$, there is $\kappa_b>0$ such that $\pi_b(X)\ge\kappa_b X$ for all sufficiently large $X$; the positive-target input comes from \cite{Mazur_Pred_2026}, while the negative-target case uses a separate $3x-1$ predecessor result. The map $T_{3,1}$ on $\mathbb Z$ preserves the sign of every nonzero integer, and by~\eqref{eq:mirror} we have $T_{3,1}(-m)=-T_{3,-1}(m)$ for every $m\ge1$. Consequently, for $X\ge a$,
\[
 \pi_a(X)=\#\bigl(P_{3,1}(a)\cap[1,X]\bigr),\qquad
 \pi_{-a}(X)=\#\bigl(P_{3,-1}(a)\cap[1,X]\bigr),
\]
where $P_{3,c}(a)$ denotes the predecessor set for the indicated map. Thus the predecessor set of $a$ has positive lower natural density for both $c=1$ and $c=-1$. Positive lower natural density implies positive lower logarithmic density; since $h(a)$ exists by Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(a), we obtain $h(a)>0$. For $3\mid a$, use Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(b). Let $A$ be completely invariant and $n\in A$. The orbit of $n$ contains a number $b=T_{3,c}(m)$ with $m$ odd, and $3\nmid b$ because $2b=3m+c$. As $b\in A$ and $P(b)\subseteq A$, we get $\delta(A)\ge h(b)>0$. The last statement follows for the complement of the three basins, which is completely invariant and has the density $1-\delta_1-\delta_5-\delta_{17}$, where $\delta_1$, $\delta_5$ and $\delta_{17}$ are the densities of the three basins.
\end{proof}

For $3x+1$, the corollary contains the criterion of \cite{Mazur_Pred_2026}: the Collatz conjecture holds if and only if the basin of $1$ has density $1$. For $3x-1$, it turns the conjecture that the three known cycles attract all integers into a statement about three numbers. Every $n\le10^{10}$ enters one of the three cycles (Proposition~\ref{prop:verification}), and the measured densities add up to $1$ within the sampling error (Table~\ref{table:passage}). Table~\ref{table:passage} rests on $10^9$ sampled starting values per map, $10^8$ in each of five windows between $10^{10}$ and $10^{220}$ for natural and for logarithmic counting, and every sampled orbit entered one of the known cycles; the same holds for the $5\cdot10^7$ starting values per map of Run~D. By the corollary, orbits of $3x-1$ that avoid the three cycles, if they exist, form a set of positive density. For each of the ten sampled windows and counting modes, the sampling bounds the proportion of such orbits by $3\cdot10^{-8}$ with $95\,\%$ confidence (rule of three). This is no statement about the density itself, for which \cite{Mazur_Pred_2026,ElJaouhari_2026} give no explicit lower bound. For the other maps of the family, the positivity of $h$ outside the multiples of $3$ is open.

\subsection*{The mean value and the Syracuse profile of the potential}
Proposition~\ref{prop:potential} does not say how large the potential is. A heuristic count gives its mean value. For a finite set $W$ of targets that are not periodic, $\sum_{a\in W}h(a)$ is the mean number of points that an orbit has in $W$, the mean taken over the starting values in the logarithmic sense. Along the orbit of a typical starting value, consecutive Syracuse iterates are related by $m'=(3m+c)/2^{\nu}$, and the valuations $\nu$ behave like independent geometric random variables of mean $2$ \cite{Tao_2022,GGM_2025}. The logarithm of the iterates decreases by $\log(4/3)$ per Syracuse step on average, and a Syracuse step consists of $\nu$ steps of $T_{3,c}$. A typical orbit therefore has $2/\log(4/3)$ points per unit of logarithmic scale, which suggests that $a\,h(a)$ has the mean value $2/\log(4/3)\approx6.95$.

The same count predicts how this mean is distributed over the residue classes modulo powers of $3$. Tao's Syracuse random variables \cite{Tao_2022} are
\[
\mathbf{Syrac}(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)=\sum_{m=1}^{n}3^{m-1}2^{-(\mathbf a_1+\dots+\mathbf a_m)}\bmod 3^n
\]
with independent geometric $\mathbf a_i$ of mean $2$. The $n$-th Syracuse iterate of a starting value under $T_{3,c}$ is congruent to $c$ times such a sum modulo $3^n$, formed with its first $n$ valuations in reversed order. The even points $2^im'$ of an orbit follow the same law, because $\mathbb P(\nu\ge k)/\mathbb E\,\nu=\mathbb P(\nu=k)$ for a geometric variable of mean $2$.

\begin{conjecture}[Syracuse profile of the potential]
\label{conj:profile}
Let $c\ge-1$ be odd with $3\nmid c$. For every $n\ge0$ and every residue $r$ modulo $3^n$,
\[
\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac1X\sum_{\substack{a\le X\\ a\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3^n)}}a\,h(a)=\frac{2}{\log(4/3)}\ \mathbb P\bigl(c\cdot\mathbf{Syrac}(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)=r\bigr).
\]
In particular, $a\,h(a)$ has the mean value $2/\log(4/3)$.
\end{conjecture}

The conjecture asserts that a single law governs the potentials of all maps of the family, and that the map enters only through the multiplication by $c$. For $n=1$ it gives the means $2/\log(4/3)$ and $4/\log(4/3)$ for the classes $a\equiv c$ and $a\equiv2c$ modulo $3$, and $0$ for the multiples of $3$, in accordance with Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(b). For $n=2$, the Syracuse variable takes the values $s=1,2,4,5,7,8$ with the probabilities $8/63$, $16/63$, $11/63$, $4/63$, $2/63$ and $22/63$.

The lower half of the conjecture can be proved, and the upper half is tied to the question of unbounded orbits.

\begin{theorem}[Lower bound for the potential]
\label{thm:lower}
Let $c\ge-1$ be odd with $3\nmid c$. For every $n\ge0$ and every residue $r$ modulo $3^n$,
\[
\liminf_{X\to\infty}\frac1X\sum_{\substack{a\le X\\ a\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3^n)}}a\,h(a)\ \ge\ \frac{2}{\log(4/3)}\ \mathbb P\bigl(c\cdot\mathbf{Syrac}(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)=r\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[What remains of Conjecture~\ref{conj:profile}]
\label{cor:remains}
Let $c\ge-1$ be odd with $3\nmid c$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item Conjecture~\ref{conj:profile} holds if and only if
	\begin{equation}
	\label{eq:upper}
	\limsup_{X\to\infty}\frac1X\sum_{a\le X}a\,h(a)\le\frac{2}{\log(4/3)}.
	\end{equation}
	\item If~\eqref{eq:upper} holds, then every $m\in\N$ with $h(m)>0$ has a bounded orbit. For $c=1$ and $c=-1$, where $h(m)>0$ for all $m$ not divisible by $3$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:dichotomy}), \eqref{eq:upper} implies that $T_{3,c}$ has no unbounded orbit.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

The proofs are given in Appendix~\ref{appx:lower}. Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} uses the same input from \cite{GGM_2025} as Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens}. The sum of $a\,h(a)$ over a set of targets is the mean, over the starting values, of the sum of the orbit points in this set. The points that an orbit collects on its first descent through a window do not change when the starting value is replaced by a first passage above the window. As in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens}, this reduces their mean to starting values just above the window. For these, the valuations along the descent are independent and geometric up to a negligible error, and the residue of a Syracuse iterate modulo $3^n$ depends only on its last $n$ valuations, which gives the Syracuse law. The points that an orbit collects when it returns to the window from far below are not controlled by this argument. They can only increase the sum, so that a lower bound results.

Part~(b) of the corollary shows that this is no defect of the method. An unbounded orbit passes through every window once more on its way up, and below $X$ the points of its ascent alone add up to at least $(2-o(1))X$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:escape-ladder}). The upper bound~\eqref{eq:upper} therefore excludes unbounded orbits of positive potential, and for $3x+1$ it contains, by Corollary~\ref{cor:dichotomy}, the statement that no Collatz trajectory diverges. Conjecture~\ref{conj:profile} is thus at least as hard as this open problem, and by part~(a) it is equivalent to the single inequality~\eqref{eq:upper} for the mean value. Appendix~\ref{sec:tail-programme} analyses this inequality further: the clipped first-passage mass has exactly the predicted mean (Proposition~\ref{prop:clipped-calibration}), it is exponentially tight outside an exceptional set of logarithmic density $O((\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0})$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:tightness}), and what remains are long but finite excursions and returns from far below (Remark~\ref{rem:tail-barrier}, Problem~\ref{prob:excursions}).

We measured the potential directly (Run~G). For $3x+1$, $3x-1$, $3x+5$ and $3x+13$, the program \path{potential.c} counts, for all targets $a\le10^6$ at once, the starting values $n\le10^{10}$ whose orbit contains $a$. For these counts, the identities of Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(b) and~(c) hold as exact integer identities, which tests the program, and the basin counts agree with Table~\ref{table:basins}. Table~\ref{table:syracuse} compares the means of $a\,h(a)$, with $h(a)$ estimated by the proportion of the starting values up to $10^{10}$, with Conjecture~\ref{conj:profile}. The overall means lie between $6.85$ and $6.97$. The class means modulo $9$ show the same six numbers for all four maps, once the classes are labelled by $a/c$, and deviate from the prediction by at most $2.4\,\%$. Modulo $81$, the median deviation over the $54$ classes is below $1\,\%$ for every map, and the largest deviation is below $6\,\%$. The deviations of about $1\,\%$ exceed the statistical error. They are an effect of the small targets and disappear at larger scales (Run~H). For each of the four maps we followed the orbits of $10^7$ random starting values between $10^{28}$ and $2\cdot10^{28}$ and collected their points; this estimates the means of $a\,h(a)$ over the targets of every decade $[10^k,10^{k+1})$ without storing any counts (\path{orbit_profile.c}). For $3x+1$ the decade means are $6.911$, $6.934$ and $6.949$ for $k=6,7,8$. Over the decades $9\le k\le25$ the four maps give $6.9515$, $6.9518$, $6.9524$ and $6.9518$, with a standard error of $0.0004$, against $2/\log(4/3)=6.9521$. In view of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}, the content of these measurements is the upper bound~\eqref{eq:upper}: at the sampled scales, the orbits collect no measurable weight when they return from far below. \subsection*{A predictor from the backward tree}
For a cycle $\mathcal C$ of $T_{3,c}$, let level $0$ consist of the predecessors of its members that are not on $\mathcal C$, and level $d+1$ of the predecessors of level $d$: the number $2y$, and $(2y-c)/3$ if this is a positive integer, which is then odd. Nodes divisible by $3$ have no odd predecessor; their subtrees are doubling chains of density $0$, and we do not expand them. Let $M_d(\mathcal C)$ be the sum of $1/x$ over the nodes $x\ge c/2$ of level $d$ that are not divisible by $3$. Below $c/2$ a node has no odd predecessor either, and counting it would only add transient spikes. The even predecessor of a node $y$ carries $1/(2y)$; an odd predecessor exists for half of the nodes, is counted with probability $2/3$ and carries $3/(2y)$. On average, therefore, the mass is the same on every level, and heuristically its average over deep levels is proportional to $\delta(\mathcal C)$, with a factor that depends only on the map. Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(c) makes the heuristic precise: $\delta(\mathcal C)=\sum_{x\in L_d(\mathcal C)}h(x)$ holds exactly on every level, and $M_d(\mathcal C)$ is this sum with $h(x)$ replaced by $1/x$. The predictor is exact up to a factor $\kappa$ if and only if $\sum_{x\in L_d(\mathcal C)}\bigl(h(x)-\kappa/x\bigr)$ tends to $0$ along the levels. Conjecture~\ref{conj:profile} suggests the value of the factor: the multiples of $3$ carry no potential, so that $x\,h(x)$ has the mean value $\tfrac32\cdot2/\log(4/3)$ on the other integers.

\begin{conjecture}[Density formula]
\label{conj:formula}
Let $c\ge-1$ be odd with $3\nmid c$. For every cycle $\mathcal C$ of $T_{3,c}$,
\[
\delta(\mathcal C)=\frac{3}{\log(4/3)}\ \lim_{D\to\infty}\frac1D\sum_{d<D}M_d(\mathcal C).
\]
\end{conjecture}

The omission of the nodes below $c/2$ concerns finitely many levels and does not affect the limit. The constant is $3/\log(4/3)\approx10.43$ for every map and every cycle; nothing is fitted or normalised.

We computed $M_d$ up to depth $70$ for the $410$ primitive cycles of the $98$ maps of Figure~\ref{fig:landscape} with at least two primitive cycles, by a depth-first search that needs no memory. As the predicted share of a cycle we take the mean mass of the levels $64$ to $69$, normalised so that the predicted shares of the cycles of each map add up to $1$, and compare it with the measured share of Figure~\ref{fig:landscape}. The prediction reproduces the measured shares with a median deviation of $4.9\,\%$, within a factor of $1.25$ for $90\,\%$ of the cycles, and it identifies the largest basin in $87$ of the $98$ maps (Figure~\ref{fig:predictor}). Shares proportional to the word length, or to the mass of level $0$ alone, miss by a factor of about $2$ in the median. The error decreases steadily with the depth, without a plateau so far (Table~\ref{table:convergence}). Normalising the prediction within the cosets of Proposition~\ref{prop:cosets} lowers the median deviation to $3.3\,\%$, and the largest basin is then correct in $91$ maps. The same data test Conjecture~\ref{conj:formula}, which needs no normalisation. Over the $410$ cycles, the ratio of the measured density to the mean mass of the levels $60$ to $69$ has the median $10.41$, with the quartiles $10.15$ and $10.86$, against $3/\log(4/3)=10.43$. The absolute prediction $\tfrac{3}{\log(4/3)}M_d$ deviates from the measured density by $3.0\,\%$ in the median and stays within a factor of $1.22$ for $90\,\%$ of the cycles; it is more accurate than the normalised one, presumably because it does not inherit the errors of the other cycles of the map.

Whether the averages of $M_d$ converge is open, and so are both conjectures. By Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(c), Conjecture~\ref{conj:formula} is a statement about the predecessor potential: on the deep levels of every backward tree, $x\,h(x)$ has the same mean value as on all integers not divisible by $3$. This is the precise form of Problem~\ref{prob:basins}(c).

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:basins}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:filtration}, the basin densities of $3x+c$ are determined by those of the cycles coprime to $c'$ in the maps $3x+c'$, $c'\mid c$. For these cycles:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \emph{Natural density.} Do the limits $\lim_{N\to\infty}d_N(\mathcal C)$ exist? If so, they equal $\delta(\mathcal C)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens}. By Theorem~\ref{thm:natural} they exist for every $c$, conditionally on \cite{Allikvere_2026}, and for $c=1$ also by Corollary~\ref{cor:natural}; an unconditional proof needs the confirmation of one of these preprints. Table~\ref{table:passage} shows no difference between natural and logarithmic counting up to $10^{220}$.
	\item \emph{Explanation.} Proposition~\ref{prop:cosets} fixes the densities of invariant coset sets, but does not in general determine the densities of individual cycles even if they are the only known cycles in their cosets. What decides between the cycles of one coset: their parity words, the arithmetic position of their members, the distribution of their backward iterates?
	\item \emph{Prediction.} Prove Conjectures~\ref{conj:profile} and~\ref{conj:formula}: the potential has the Syracuse profile on average, and the level averages of the backward mass $M_d(\mathcal C)$ converge to $\delta(\mathcal C)\log(4/3)/3$. Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} proves the lower half of Conjecture~\ref{conj:profile}; what remains is the upper bound~\eqref{eq:upper} for the mean value, which excludes unbounded orbits of positive potential (Corollary~\ref{cor:remains}). Numerically, the class means of $a\,h(a)$ agree with the Syracuse law to four digits for large targets (Table~\ref{table:syracuse}), and the absolute prediction reproduces the measured densities to about $3\,\%$ (Table~\ref{table:convergence}).
\end{enumerate}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:local}, any answer to (b) and (c) must use global information. For $3x+1$, the conjecture asserts a single basin of density $1$.
\end{problem}




\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:outlook}
%=====================================================================
The local and the global view answer different questions. Cayley trees and their congruence classes describe how every forest of the family branches. Parity words and the numbers $2^l-q^k$ decide how many trees a forest has and where their cycles sit. We see six directions, in order of priority:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
	\item \emph{The values of the basin densities} (Problem~\ref{prob:basins}). Existence is settled in the logarithmic sense, Proposition~\ref{prop:cosets} fixes invariant-class densities (but not generally individual basin densities), and the backward mass predicts measured basin shares to about $3\,\%$, with the explicit constant $3/\log(4/3)$ (Conjecture~\ref{conj:formula}). The central open question is why: a proof that the level averages of the backward mass converge to the densities would turn this heuristic into a formula. For the mean value of the potential, the lower bound is proved (Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}), and the matching upper bound is at least as hard as the exclusion of unbounded orbits (Corollary~\ref{cor:remains}). Remark~\ref{rem:passage} expresses each density as a first-passage probability, which may be the route to such a proof and already allows numerical estimates far beyond brute-force enumeration (Table~\ref{table:passage}). Natural densities follow for every $c$ once \cite{Allikvere_2026} is confirmed (Theorem~\ref{thm:natural}); a further aim is the features of a cycle that determine the values. This is the direction with the largest potential for new theory, independently of the Collatz conjecture itself.
	\item \emph{The upper bound for the potential} (Appendix~\ref{sec:tail-programme}): the deficit in~\eqref{eq:upper} splits into a clipping tail $T_X$ and a return term $R_X$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:two-tails}). Since the first-passage mass is exponentially tight up to a logarithmic exceptional set (Proposition~\ref{prop:tightness}), $T_X=o(X)$ reduces to Problem~\ref{prob:excursions}; a moment bound growing like $(\log X)^A$ with $A<\varepsilon_0$ would suffice (Corollary~\ref{cor:extreme-tail}), and so would uniform integrability of the stopped potential $a\,h(a)$ of the odd targets (Proposition~\ref{prop:potential-domination}). Certificates that use only a fixed residue cannot work (Proposition~\ref{prop:residue-obstruction}), and $R_X=o(X)$ is a separate open problem.
	\item \emph{The arithmetic structure of the primitive cycles} (Problem~\ref{prob:heuristic}): why the equidistribution heuristic works for coprime types, how the algebraic factors of $2^l-3^k$ bias it, and whether the counts are underdispersed.
	\item \emph{Longer words}: extending Proposition~\ref{prop:rational} to $l\le44$ and beyond, and the role of the convergents of $\log_2 3$, such as $65/41$.
	\item \emph{A Steiner-type theorem for Ba\v{r}ina's $7x\pm1$} \cite{Barina_2022}: no non-trivial circuits, via Diophantine approximation of $\log_2 7$ and Baker-type bounds, in the spirit of \cite{Steiner_1977,Simons_deWeger_2005}.
	\item \emph{Certification}: finite predecessor, hitting, clipping, return-pair and return-Lyapunov identities are kernel-checked in Lean~4 (Section~\ref{sec:contributions}); extending this to the analytic moment hypotheses, the valuation coupling and the Syracuse-profile lower bound remains future work.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Supplementary remarks on cycles}
\label{appx:cycles}
\begin{remark}[The case $q=1$]
For $q=1$ we have $z_1(B)=\sum_j 2^{p_j}$, the integer whose binary digits, least significant first, are $b_0,\ldots,b_{l-1}$. For $c=2^l-1$, the map $T_{1,c}$ acts on $\{0,\ldots,2^l-1\}$ as the cyclic right rotation of $l$-bit numbers. For example, the cycles of $x+7$ are $\{1,4,2\}$, $\{3,5,6\}$ and $\{7\}$, the rotation classes of $001$, $011$ and $111$. For $c=1$, by contrast, $0<z_1(B)<2^l-1$ unless $B=1^l$, so $T_{1,1}$ has only the cycle $\{1\}$. This also follows from the descent $(x+1)/2<x$ for odd $x>1$, a global, Archimedean argument. This is the trivial base case $1n+1$ of the family $an+b$.
\end{remark}

\paragraph{The product identity.}
Let $a_1,\ldots,a_k$ be the odd members of a positive cycle of~\eqref{eq:func_qxplus1} of type $(l,k)$, in cyclic order, and put $A_i=qa_i$. Dividing~\eqref{eq:condition_cycle_existance} by $q^k$ gives
\[
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(1+\frac1{A_i}\Bigr)=\frac{2^l}{q^k},
\]
and for odd $q>1$ the right-hand side determines $(l,k)$. Hence the members of two cycles satisfy the product equality
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diophantine_equation}
(A_1+1)\cdots(A_m+1)\cdot B_1\cdots B_n=A_1\cdots A_m\cdot(B_1+1)\cdots(B_n+1)
\end{equation}
if and only if the cycles have the same type. For example, the two non-trivial cycles of $5x+1$ both have type $(7,3)$, so~\eqref{eq:diophantine_equation} holds for $A=5\cdot(13,33,83)$ and $B=5\cdot(27,17,43)$; both products equal $2^7/5^3$. Likewise, both cycles of $181x+1$ have type $(15,2)$ (Table~\ref{table:zoo}).

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} and Corollary~\ref{cor:remains}}
\label{appx:lower}

Throughout this appendix, $c\ge-1$ is odd with $3\nmid c$, and we put $\gamma_0=2/\log(4/3)$, $\mu_0=\log(4/3)$ and $p_r=\mathbb P(c\cdot\mathbf{Syrac}(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)=r)$. The letter $C$ denotes positive constants that may change from line to line. They may depend on $c$, on $n$ and on the parameters $\alpha$, $\lambda_0$ and $\beta$ fixed below, but not on $X$.

For an odd $m$ write $m_j=\operatorname{Syr}^j(m)$, $a_j=a_j(m)=\nu_2(3m_{j-1}+c)$ and $A_j=a_1+\dots+a_j$. The orbit of $m$ under $T_{3,c}$ consists of $m$ and of the numbers $2^im_j$ with $j\ge1$ and $0\le i<a_j$; we call these $a_j$ numbers the $j$-th \emph{block}. The orbit runs through the blocks in the order of $j$, and through each block in the order of decreasing $i$. By induction on $j$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:iterate}
m_j=\frac{3^jm+c\sum_{t=1}^{j}3^{j-t}2^{A_{t-1}}}{2^{A_j}} .
\end{equation}
Hence, for $j\ge n$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:residue}
m_j\equiv c\,\sigma_j\pmod{3^n},\qquad\sigma_j=\sum_{t=1}^{n}3^{t-1}2^{-(a_{j-t+1}+\dots+a_j)},
\end{equation}
and, with $m_j^*=3^jm/2^{A_j}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ideal}
\frac{m_j}{m_j^*}-1=\frac c3\sum_{t=0}^{j-1}\frac1{m_t^*}.
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Reduction to a window just above the targets]
\label{lem:reduction}
Let $\varepsilon_0$, $\varepsilon_1$ and $\lambda_0$ be as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens}, and let $\alpha\in(1,1+\varepsilon_1)$. There is a constant $C$ with the following property. Let $x_0\ge2$, and let $\varphi$ be a function from the odd positive integers to $[0,1]$ such that $\varphi(m)=\varphi(\operatorname{Pass}_x(m))$ for all $x\ge x_0$ and all odd $m>x$ with $\tau_x(m)<\infty$ and $\operatorname{Pass}_x(m)>\tfrac34x\exp(-(\log x)^{0.7})$. Then the limit
\[
\delta_\varphi=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1{\log N}\sum_{w<N}\frac{\varphi(w^\flat)}{w}
\]
exists, where $w^\flat$ denotes the odd part of $w$, and
\[
\bigl|\mathbb E\,\varphi(\mathbf{Log}_{y,y^\alpha})-\delta_\varphi\bigr|\le C(\log y)^{-\varepsilon_0}\qquad\text{for all }y\ge x_0^{\lambda_0}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $F_\alpha(y)=\mathbb E\,\varphi(\mathbf{Log}_{y,y^\alpha})$. Let $y\ge x_0^{\lambda_0}$ and $x=y^{1/\lambda_0}\ge x_0$. For each of the two windows of Step~3 of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens}, with $\mathbf m$ distributed logarithmically on the window, the hypothesis on $\varphi$ gives
\begin{multline*}
\bigl|\mathbb E\,\varphi(\mathbf m)-\mathbb E\,\varphi(\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf m))\bigr|\\
\le\mathbb P\bigl(\tau_x(\mathbf m)=\infty\bigr)+\mathbb P\Bigl(\tau_x(\mathbf m)<\infty,\ \operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf m)\le\tfrac34x\exp\bigl(-(\log x)^{0.7}\bigr)\Bigr),
\end{multline*}
which is $\ll(\log x)^{-\varepsilon_0}$ by~\eqref{eq:ggm_hit} and~\eqref{eq:ggm_low2}. With~\eqref{eq:ggm_tv} this yields $|F_\alpha(y)-F_\alpha(y^\alpha)|\ll(\log y)^{-\varepsilon_0}$ for all $y\ge x_0^{\lambda_0}$, with an implied constant that depends neither on $\varphi$ nor on $x_0$. Steps~4 to~6 of that proof use the set $A_1$ only through this inequality, through the bounds $0\le\mathbf 1_{A_1}\le1$, and through the identity $\sum_{m\in[y,y^\alpha),\,m\text{ odd}}\mathbf 1_{A_1}(m)/m=F_\alpha(y)\,\mu([y,y^\alpha))$. They apply to $\varphi$ in place of $\mathbf 1_{A_1}$ and show that $\mu([1,Y))^{-1}\sum_{m<Y,\,m\text{ odd}}\varphi(m)/m$ converges to a limit $\delta_\varphi$, and that the periodic function $\psi_\alpha$ of Step~4 is the constant $\delta_\varphi$. The estimate $|f_\alpha(t)-\psi_\alpha(t)|\ll e^{-\varepsilon_0t}$ of Step~4, which now holds for $t\ge\log\log x_0^{\lambda_0}$, is the asserted bound. The formula for $\delta_\varphi$ follows as in Step~1.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Valuations in a logarithmic window]
\label{lem:valuations}
Let $u\ge2$ and $v\ge4u$, and let $k\ge1$ be an integer with $2^k\le u$. On a suitable probability space there are a random variable $\mathbf N$ with the law of $\mathbf{Log}_{u,v}$, a random variable $U$ that is uniformly distributed on $[\log u,\log v)$, and independent random variables $g_1,g_2,\ldots$ with $\mathbb P(g_j=i)=2^{-i}$ for $i\ge1$, independent of $U$, such that, outside an event of probability at most $C2^k/u$,
\[
|\log\mathbf N-U|\le2^k/u\qquad\text{and}\qquad a_j(\mathbf N)=g_j\ \text{ for all $j$ with }g_1+\dots+g_j\le k-1 .
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Cut $[u,v)$ into the intervals $B_s=[u+s2^k,u+(s+1)2^k)$, $0\le s<S$, and a rest of length less than $2^k$. Each $B_s$ contains exactly one member of each odd residue class modulo $2^k$, and the reciprocals of its members differ by a factor of at most $1+2^k/u$. Choose $s$ with a probability proportional to $\int_{B_s}dt/t$, choose an odd residue $\rho$ modulo $2^k$ uniformly and independently of $s$, let $\mathbf N'$ be the member of $B_s$ in the class $\rho$, and, given $s$, let $U'$ be uniformly distributed on $\log B_s$ and independent of $\rho$. Then $U'$ is uniformly distributed on $[\log u,\log(u+S2^k))$ and independent of $\rho$, and $|\log\mathbf N'-U'|\le2^k/u$. A comparison of the point masses shows that the law of $\mathbf N'$ and the law of $\mathbf{Log}_{u,v}$ differ in total variation by $\ll2^k/u$, and so do the law of $U'$ and the uniform distribution on $[\log u,\log v)$.

The valuations $a_1,\ldots,a_j$ of an odd integer depend only on its residue modulo $2^{A_j+1}$. If $\rho$ is uniformly distributed, then $a_1(\rho)=b_1$ has the probability $2^{-b_1}$ for $b_1\le k-1$, and under this condition $(3\rho+c)/2^{b_1}$ is uniformly distributed on the odd residues modulo $2^{k-b_1}$. By induction, the first $j$ valuations of $\rho$ are $b_1,\ldots,b_j$ with the probability $2^{-(b_1+\dots+b_j)}$ whenever $b_1+\dots+b_j\le k-1$. These are the probabilities of the same events for independent $g_j$ with the stated law. Hence the $g_j$ can be defined on the same probability space, independently of $s$ and $U'$, such that $a_j(\mathbf N')=a_j(\rho)=g_j$ for all $j$ with $g_1+\dots+g_j\le k-1$. Finally we couple $\mathbf N'$ with a variable $\mathbf N$ of the law of $\mathbf{Log}_{u,v}$, and $U'$ with a uniform variable $U$ on $[\log u,\log v)$, such that $\mathbf N\ne\mathbf N'$ or $U\ne U'$ has a probability $\ll2^k/u$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[The model]
\label{lem:model}
Let $n\ge1$, let $U$ be uniformly distributed on $[s,s+L]$ with $L\ge1$, and let $g_1,g_2,\ldots$ be independent of $U$ and of each other, with $\mathbb P(g_j=i)=2^{-i}$ for $i\ge1$. Put $D_j=\sum_{t\le j}(g_t\log2-\log3)$ and $z_{j,i}=U-D_j+i\log2$ for $j\ge1$ and $0\le i<g_j$, and $\sigma_j=\sum_{t=1}^{n}3^{t-1}2^{-(g_{j-t+1}+\dots+g_j)}$ for $j\ge n$. Let $\ell_0<\ell_1<\ell_2\le s$, let $\tau^*=\inf\{j\ge0:U-D_j\le\ell_0\}$ and
\[
Z=\sum_{j=n}^{\tau^*}\ \sum_{i=0}^{g_j-1}e^{z_{j,i}}\,\mathbf 1\bigl[\ell_1<z_{j,i}\le\ell_2,\ c\,2^i\sigma_j\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3^n)\bigr].
\]
Then
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item $\mathbb E\,Z\ge(\gamma_0p_r-C/L)(e^{\ell_2}-e^{\ell_1})-Ce^{\ell_2-(\ell_1-\ell_0)}(1+\ell_2-\ell_1)$,
	\item $\mathbb E\,Z^2\le Ce^{2\ell_2}$,
	\item $\mathbb P\bigl(g_1+\dots+g_{\tau^*}>24\Delta+3\bigr)\le Ce^{-\Delta/C}$, where $\Delta=s+L-\ell_0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The steps $\xi_t=g_t\log2-\log3$ have the mean $\mu_0$ and exponential moments, and $\mathbb E\,e^{-\xi_t}=3\sum_{i\ge1}4^{-i}=1$. We use three standard facts about the random walk $(D_j)$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
	\item For $t\ge0$ let $T_t=\inf\{j\ge0:D_j\ge t\}$. Then $\mu_0\,\mathbb E\,T_t=\mathbb E\,D_{T_t}$ by Wald's identity, and $t\le\mathbb E\,D_{T_t}\le t+C$ by Lorden's inequality \cite{Lorden_1970}.
	\item By Chernoff's bound,
$\mathbb P(D_j<w)\le e^{w/2}\varrho^j$ with
$\varrho=\mathbb E\,e^{-\xi_t/2}<1$.
Here is a uniform visit bound that will also control second moments.
For any unit interval $I=[q,q+1)$, let $J$ be its first visit
($J=\infty$ if there is none). On $\{J<\infty\}$, conditional on
$\mathcal F_J$, the number of later visits to $I$ is bounded by
$\sum_{i\ge1}\mathbf 1[D'_i<1]$, where $(D'_i)$ is an independent
copy of the walk. In particular,
\[
\mathbb E\#\{j\ge0:D_j\in I\}
\le1+e^{1/2}\sum_{i\ge1}\varrho^i\le C.
\]
By Minkowski's inequality, the same unit-interval visit count
has a uniformly bounded second moment, since
$\sum_{i\ge1}\mathbb P(D'_i<1)^{1/2}<\infty$.
Covering $[q,q+\ell)$ by at most $\lceil\ell\rceil+1$ unit intervals
shows that the expected number of visits to this interval is at most
$C(1+\ell)$. The same estimates hold for the walk after any
stopping time, conditionally on the past, by the strong Markov property.
	\item $(e^{-D_j})_{j\ge0}$ is a nonnegative martingale with mean $1$, so that $\mathbb P(\inf_jD_j\le-w)\le e^{-w}$ for $w\ge0$.
\end{enumerate}
In the coordinate $y=U-z$, the points of the $j$-th block are $y_{j,i}=D_j-i\log2$. They lie in $[D_{j-1}-\log\tfrac32,\,D_j]$, because $D_j-(g_j-1)\log2=D_{j-1}+\log2-\log3$. Let $m_j$ be the number of $i<g_j$ with $c\,2^i\sigma_j\equiv r$ modulo $3^n$. It is a function of $g_{j-n+1},\ldots,g_j$ with $0\le m_j\le g_j$. We have $2^i\sigma_j=(1+3\sigma')2^{-(g_j-i)}$ with $\sigma'=\sum_{t=1}^{n-1}3^{t-1}2^{-(g_{j-t}+\dots+g_{j-1})}$, which is independent of $g_j$, and $(1+3\sigma')2^{-g_j}$ has the law of $\mathbf{Syrac}(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)$. Hence
\[
\mathbb E\,m_j=\sum_{i\ge1}\mathbb P(g_j\ge i)\,\mathbb P\bigl(c(1+3\sigma')2^{-i}\equiv r\bigr)=2\sum_{i\ge1}2^{-i}\,\mathbb P\bigl(c(1+3\sigma')2^{-i}\equiv r\bigr)=2p_r .
\]
For $t\ge0$ let $V(t)$ be the expected number of pairs $(j,i)$ with $j\ge n$, $c\,2^i\sigma_j\equiv r$ and $y_{j,i}<t$. We claim that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:V}
|V(t)-\gamma_0p_rt|\le C\qquad\text{for all }t\ge0 .
\end{equation}
Write $T=T_t$. The blocks $j<T$ lie below $t$, and a block $j>T$ has points below $t$ only if $D_{j-1}-D_T<\log\tfrac32$. Hence
\[
\mathbb E\sum_{j=n}^{T-1}m_j\ \le\ V(t)\ \le\ \mathbb E\sum_{j=n}^{T}m_j+\mathbb E\sum_{j>T}g_j\,\mathbf 1\bigl[D_{j-1}-D_T<\log\tfrac32\bigr],
\]
and the last expectation is $2\sum_{i\ge0}\mathbb P(D_i<\log\tfrac32)\le C$ by~(ii). The event $\{T\ge j-n+1\}$ depends only on $g_1,\ldots,g_{j-n}$ and is independent of $m_j$. Therefore
\[
\mathbb E\sum_{j=n}^{T}m_j=\sum_{j\ge n}\mathbb E\bigl[m_j\mathbf 1[T\ge j-n+1]\bigr]-\sum_{j\ge n}\mathbb E\bigl[m_j\mathbf 1[j-n+1\le T\le j-1]\bigr]=2p_r\,\mathbb E\,T-\vartheta
\]
with $0\le\vartheta\le\mathbb E\sum_{j=T+1}^{T+n-1}g_j=2(n-1)$. Moreover,
\[
\mathbb E\,m_T\le\mathbb E\,g_T\le\sum_{i\ge1}i\,2^{-i}\,\mathbb E\,\#\bigl\{j\ge1:\ t-i\log2+\log3\le D_{j-1}<t\bigr\}\le C
\]
by~(ii). With~(i) and $\gamma_0=2/\mu_0$, this proves~\eqref{eq:V}.

(a) Since $U=z_{j,i}+y_{j,i}$ is uniformly distributed on $[s,s+L]$ and independent of the $g_j$,
\[
\mathbb E\sum_{j\ge n}\ \sum_{i=0}^{g_j-1}e^{z_{j,i}}\,\mathbf 1\bigl[\ell_1<z_{j,i}\le\ell_2,\ c\,2^i\sigma_j\equiv r\bigr]=\frac1L\int_{\ell_1}^{\ell_2}e^z\bigl(V(s-z+L)-V(s-z)\bigr)\,dz .
\]
As $s-z\ge0$, the integrand is at least $e^z(\gamma_0p_rL-2C)$ by~\eqref{eq:V}, which gives the first term of~(a). The sum $Z$ omits the blocks $j>\tau^*$. Such a block can have a point above $\ell_1$ only if
$D_{j-1}<b:=U-\ell_1+\log\tfrac32$, while
$D_{\tau^*}\ge U-\ell_0$. Put
$d=\ell_1-\ell_0-\log\tfrac32$ and let $\sigma$ be the first
$k\ge1$ with $D_{\tau^*+k}<b$ (if no such $k$ exists, no
omitted block contributes). For $d>0$, the exponential martingale
in~(iii), applied after $\tau^*$, gives
$\mathbb P(\sigma<\infty)\le e^{-d}
=\tfrac32e^{-(\ell_1-\ell_0)}$.
The negative increment is always $-\log\tfrac32$ (it occurs when
$g_j=1$); thus, on $\{\sigma<\infty\}$, the first return ends
in $[b-\log\tfrac32,b)$, and no omitted block can contribute
before the next increment. By the strong Markov property at this
first return, a later block with $g_j=i$ and a point in
$(\ell_1,\ell_2]$ must satisfy
$U-\ell_2-i\log2\le D_{j-1}<b$. The uniform interval-visit
estimate in~(ii), conditional on the return, bounds its expected
number of points by
$\sum_{i\ge1}i\,2^{-i}C(1+\ell_2-\ell_1+i)
\le C(1+\ell_2-\ell_1)$.
Each point has weight at most $e^{\ell_2}$; multiplying by the
probability of the return proves the error term in~(a).
If $d\le0$, the same conditional interval-visit estimate applied
directly after $\tau^*$ gives an error
$O(e^{\ell_2}(1+\ell_2-\ell_1))$, which is absorbed in the
stated error term because
$e^{-(\ell_1-\ell_0)}\ge 2/3$ in this case.

(b) For $q\le s$ let $N_q$ be the number of points $z_{j,i}$ in $(q-1,q]$, and let $j_1\ge1$ be the first $j$ with $U-D_j\le q$. A block $j<j_1$ has no point in $(q-1,q]$, and a block $j>j_1$ has one only if $D_{j-1}-D_{j_1}<1+\log\tfrac32$. Hence $N_q\le g_{j_1}+\sum_{i\ge1}g_{j_1+i}\mathbf 1[D_{j_1+i-1}-D_{j_1}<1+\log\tfrac32]$, where $g_{j_1+i}$ is independent of the indicator next to it. The overshooting valuation also has a uniformly bounded second
moment. Indeed, for $t=U-q>0$, conditioning on $g_j=v$ and using
that $j$ is a first crossing only if
$t-v\log2+\log3\le D_{j-1}<t$, we obtain by~(ii)
\[
\mathbb E\,g_{j_1}^2
\le\sum_{v\ge1}v^2 2^{-v}
\mathbb E\#\{j\ge1:t-v\log2+\log3\le D_{j-1}<t\}
\le C\sum_{v\ge1}v^2 2^{-v}(1+v)\le C.
\]
After the stopping time $j_1$, the variables $g_{j_1+i}$ are
independent of the events
$\{D_{j_1+i-1}-D_{j_1}<1+\log\tfrac32\}$.
Minkowski's inequality and the Chernoff bound in~(ii) consequently give
\[
\|N_q\|_{L^2}
\le C+(\mathbb E g_1^2)^{1/2}
\sum_{i\ge1}\mathbb P(D_{i-1}<1+\log\tfrac32)^{1/2}
\le C,
\]
uniformly in $q\le s$. Since
$Z\le\sum_{i\ge0}e^{\ell_2-i}N_{\ell_2-i}$, a second
application of Minkowski's inequality gives~(b).

(c) Let $j_0=\lceil8\Delta\rceil$. If $\tau^*>j_0$, then $D_{j_0}<\Delta$. We have $\mathbb E\,D_{j_0}\ge2\Delta$ and $\mathbb E(g_1+\dots+g_{j_0})=2j_0$, so that $\mathbb P(D_{j_0}<\Delta)$ and $\mathbb P(g_1+\dots+g_{j_0}>3j_0)$ are at most $Ce^{-\Delta/C}$ by Chernoff's bound. As $3j_0\le24\Delta+3$, this proves~(c).
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}]
We may assume $n\ge1$, because the case $n=0$ follows from the case $n=1$ by summation over the three residues modulo $3$. Let $\beta=1/200$, and choose $\lambda_0$ and $\alpha$ in $(1,1+\varepsilon_1)$ with $\lambda_0\alpha\le1+\beta$; the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} allows every such choice. Let $X$ be large, and put
\[
W=\{a\in(X^{1-\beta},X]:\ a\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3^n)\},\qquad X_1=X^{1-2\beta},\qquad x_0=X^{1+\beta}.
\]
For an odd $m$ let $\tau=\tau_{X_1}(m)$, and let $\Phi(m)$ be the sum of the members of $W$ among the numbers of the blocks $1,\ldots,\tau$ of $m$, counted with multiplicity, if $\tau<\infty$, and $\Phi(m)=0$ if $\tau=\infty$.

\emph{Step 1 (comparison with the potential).} Let $\tau<\infty$. No number occurs twice in the blocks $1,\ldots,\tau$: otherwise the orbit would be periodic from the first of two equal numbers on, and the odd number $m_\tau$ would occur before the end of the block $\tau$, that is, as a Syracuse iterate $m_j$ with $j<\tau$; this is impossible, because $m_\tau$ is the first Syracuse iterate that does not exceed $X_1$. Hence $\Phi(m)\le\sum_{a\in W,\ a\in\operatorname{orb}(m)}a$ for every odd $m$, where $\operatorname{orb}(m)$ denotes the orbit of $m$ as a set. By Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(a) and since $W$ is finite,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mean_of_points}
\sum_{a\in W}a\,h(a)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1{\log N}\sum_{w<N}\frac1w\sum_{a\in W,\ a\in\operatorname{orb}(w)}a\ \ge\ \limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac1{\log N}\sum_{w<N}\frac{\Phi(w^\flat)}w,
\end{equation}
because the orbit of $w$ contains the orbit of its odd part $w^\flat$.

\emph{Step 2 (invariance under first passage).} Let $x\ge x_0$, let $m>x$ be odd with $t=\tau_x(m)<\infty$, and let $M=\operatorname{Pass}_x(m)=m_t$. The blocks of $M$ are the blocks $t+1,t+2,\ldots$ of $m$, and $\tau_{X_1}(m)=t+\tau_{X_1}(M)$. The numbers $2^im_j$ of the blocks $j\le t$ are at least $m_j$, and $m_j>x>X$ for $j<t$. Hence $\Phi(m)=\Phi(M)$ whenever $M>X$. For large $X$ we have $\tfrac34x\exp(-(\log x)^{0.7})\ge X$ for all $x\ge x_0$. Let $M_X=(\log X)^{\varepsilon_0/2}$. Then $\varphi=\min(\Phi,M_XX)/(M_XX)$ satisfies the hypothesis of Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction}. With $u=x_0^{\lambda_0}$, $v=u^\alpha$ and $\mathbf N=\mathbf{Log}_{u,v}$, Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction} and~\eqref{eq:mean_of_points} give
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reduced}
\sum_{a\in W}a\,h(a)\ \ge\ M_XX\,\delta_\varphi\ \ge\ \mathbb E\min\bigl(\Phi(\mathbf N),M_XX\bigr)-CX(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0/2}.
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 3 (the window above the targets).} We have $X^{1+\beta}<u<v\le X^{(1+\beta)^2}$. Let $\varepsilon=X^{-1/2}$ and $k=\lfloor\log_2\sqrt X\rfloor$. We apply Lemma~\ref{lem:valuations} with these $u$, $v$ and $k$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:model} with the resulting $U$ and $g_j$, with $s=\log u$, $L=\log(v/u)$ and
\[
\ell_0=\log(3X_1),\qquad\ell_1=\log\bigl((1+3\varepsilon)X^{1-\beta}\bigr),\qquad\ell_2=\log\bigl((1-3\varepsilon)X\bigr).
\]
Let $\tau^{**}=\inf\{j\ge0:U-D_j\le\log(X_1/2)\}\ge\tau^*$, and let $G$ be the event that the statements of Lemma~\ref{lem:valuations} hold and that $g_1+\dots+g_{\tau^{**}}\le k-1$. The number $\Delta'=\log v-\log(X_1/2)$ satisfies $3\beta\log X\le\Delta'\le(4\beta+\beta^2)\log X+1$, so that $24\Delta'+3\le k-1$ for large $X$. Lemma~\ref{lem:valuations} and Lemma~\ref{lem:model}(c), applied with $\log(X_1/2)$ in place of $\ell_0$, give $\mathbb P(G^c)\le CX^{-1/C}$.

On $G$ we have $a_j(\mathbf N)=g_j$ for all $j\le\tau^{**}$, and $\tau^{**}\le k$. Write $\mathbf N_j=\operatorname{Syr}^j(\mathbf N)$ and $\mathbf N_j^*=3^j\mathbf N/2^{A_j}$, so that $\log\mathbf N_j^*=\log\mathbf N-D_j$ for $j\le\tau^{**}$, and recall $|\log\mathbf N-U|\le2^k/u\le\varepsilon$. For $j<\tau^{**}$ we have $U-D_j>\log(X_1/2)$, hence $\mathbf N_j^*>X_1/3$. By~\eqref{eq:ideal}, $|\mathbf N_j/\mathbf N_j^*-1|\le|c|k/X_1\le\varepsilon$ for all $j\le\tau^{**}$. Therefore $\mathbf N_j\le(1+\varepsilon)e^{\varepsilon}X_1/2<X_1$ for $j=\tau^{**}$, and $\mathbf N_j\ge(1-\varepsilon)e^{-\varepsilon}\,3X_1>X_1$ for $j<\tau^*$, that is,
\[
\tau^*\le\tau_{X_1}(\mathbf N)\le\tau^{**}<\infty .
\]
Let $(j,i)$ be a pair that contributes to $Z$. Then $j\le\tau^*\le\tau_{X_1}(\mathbf N)$, and $2^i\mathbf N_j^*=\exp(z_{j,i}+\log\mathbf N-U)$ lies between $(1+3\varepsilon)e^{-\varepsilon}X^{1-\beta}$ and $(1-3\varepsilon)e^{\varepsilon}X$. Hence $X^{1-\beta}<2^i\mathbf N_j\le X$ and $2^i\mathbf N_j\ge(1-2\varepsilon)e^{z_{j,i}}$, and $2^i\mathbf N_j\equiv c\,2^i\sigma_j\equiv r$ modulo $3^n$ by~\eqref{eq:residue}. Thus $2^i\mathbf N_j$ is a member of $W$ in one of the blocks $1,\ldots,\tau_{X_1}(\mathbf N)$ of $\mathbf N$, different pairs belong to different places of these blocks, and
\[
\Phi(\mathbf N)\ge(1-2\varepsilon)Z\qquad\text{on }G .
\]
Consequently,
\begin{align*}
\mathbb E\min\bigl(\Phi(\mathbf N),M_XX\bigr)&\ge(1-2\varepsilon)\Bigl(\mathbb E\,Z-\mathbb E\bigl[Z\,\mathbf 1_{G^c}\bigr]-\mathbb E\bigl[Z\,\mathbf 1[Z>M_XX]\bigr]\Bigr)\\
&\ge(1-2\varepsilon)\Bigl(\mathbb E\,Z-\bigl(\mathbb E\,Z^2\,\mathbb P(G^c)\bigr)^{1/2}-\frac{\mathbb E\,Z^2}{M_XX}\Bigr).
\end{align*}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:model}(a) and~(b), with $L\ge(\alpha-1)\log X$, $e^{\ell_2}-e^{\ell_1}=X+O(X^{1-\beta})$, $e^{-(\ell_1-\ell_0)}\le3X^{-\beta}$ and $\ell_2-\ell_1\le\beta\log X$, we get $\mathbb E\,Z\ge\gamma_0p_rX-CX/\log X$ and $\mathbb E\,Z^2\le CX^2$. Hence $\mathbb E\min(\Phi(\mathbf N),M_XX)\ge\gamma_0p_rX-o(X)$, and by~\eqref{eq:reduced}
\[
\sum_{\substack{a\le X\\ a\equiv r\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3^n)}}a\,h(a)\ \ge\ \sum_{a\in W}a\,h(a)\ \ge\ \gamma_0p_rX-o(X).\qedhere
\]
\end{proof}


For part~(b) of Corollary~\ref{cor:remains} we use an elementary property of unbounded orbits.

\begin{lemma}[Escape ladder]
\label{lem:escape-ladder}
Let $c\ge-1$ be odd, and suppose that the orbit $O$ of $m_0\in\N$ under $T_{3,c}$ is unbounded. For every $\rho>3/2$ there is $K=K(\rho,c,m_0)$ such that, for all sufficiently large $X$, the orbit $O$ contains distinct numbers $p_0,\ldots,p_J\in(m_0,X]$, all occurring after $m_0$, with
\[
 p_0+\dots+p_J\ \ge\ \frac{X}{\rho-1}-K .
\]
In particular, $\liminf_{X\to\infty}X^{-1}\sum_{a\le X,\ a\in O}a\ge2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $d=\max\{c,0\}/2$, so that $T_{3,c}(n)\le\frac32n+d$ for all $n$. Fix $B>\max\{m_0,1,d/(\rho-\frac32)\}$, put $b_j=X\rho^{-(j+1)}$, and let $J$ be maximal with $b_J\ge B$. Since $O$ is unbounded and starts at $m_0<b_j$, it has a first member $p_j>b_j$, and $p_j$ occurs after $m_0$. Its predecessor in $O$ is at most $b_j$, so that $b_j<p_j\le\frac32b_j+d\le\rho b_j$. The intervals $(b_j,\rho b_j]$ are pairwise disjoint and contained in $(B,X]$, so the $p_j$ are distinct, exceed $m_0$ and are at most $X$. Since $b_{J+1}=X\rho^{-(J+2)}<B$,
\[
 \sum_{j=0}^Jp_j>\sum_{j=0}^Jb_j=\frac{X}{\rho-1}\bigl(1-\rho^{-(J+1)}\bigr)>\frac{X}{\rho-1}-\frac{\rho B}{\rho-1}.
\]
Divide by $X$, let $X\to\infty$, and then let $\rho\downarrow\frac32$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:remains}]
(a) For $n=0$, Conjecture~\ref{conj:profile} states that $X^{-1}\sum_{a\le X}a\,h(a)\to\gamma_0$, which contains~\eqref{eq:upper}. Conversely, assume~\eqref{eq:upper}, and let $n\ge0$ and $r$ be given. The sum over the class of $r$ is the sum over all $a\le X$ minus the sums over the other classes modulo $3^n$. By~\eqref{eq:upper}, by Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} for the other classes, and since the $p_{r'}$ add up to $1$, its upper limit after division by $X$ is at most $\gamma_0-\gamma_0(1-p_r)=\gamma_0p_r$. With Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} for $r$ itself, the limit exists and equals $\gamma_0p_r$.

(b) Suppose that $h(m_0)>0$ and that the orbit $O$ of $m_0$ is unbounded. Then no number occurs twice in $O$, and $T_{3,c}^k(m_0)\to\infty$. We use the notation of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} with $n=1$. For the three residues $r$ modulo $3$ let $\Phi_r$ and $\varphi_r$ be the functions $\Phi$ and $\varphi$ of that proof, and let $\Phi=\Phi_0+\Phi_1+\Phi_2$; thus $\Phi(m)$ is the sum of the numbers in $(X^{1-\beta},X]$ in the blocks $1,\ldots,\tau_{X_1}(m)$ of $m$. Fix $\rho>3/2$, take $X$ so large that $X_1\ge m_0$ and Lemma~\ref{lem:escape-ladder} applies, let $p_0,\ldots,p_J$ be the numbers it provides, and put $\Sigma_X=p_0+\dots+p_J\ge X/(\rho-1)-K$. Let $w\in P(m_0)$. The orbit of $w$ contains $O$; it is not eventually periodic, so that all its members are different. We claim that
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:extra}
\sum_{a\le X,\ a\in\operatorname{orb}(w)}a\ \ge\ \Phi(w^\flat)+\Sigma_X .
\end{equation}
This is clear if $\Phi(w^\flat)=0$. Otherwise $\tau=\tau_{X_1}(w^\flat)$ is finite and positive, and $m_0$ lies on the orbit of $w^\flat$, because $w^\flat>X_1\ge m_0$. The first Syracuse iterate of $w^\flat$ that does not exceed $X_1$ is reached at the latest with the odd part of $m_0$. The members of the blocks $1,\ldots,\tau$ of $w^\flat$ therefore either precede $m_0$ in the orbit or are at most $m_0$, whereas every $p_j$ follows $m_0$ and exceeds it. Hence no $p_j$ is counted in $\Phi(w^\flat)$, which proves~\eqref{eq:extra}. For $w\notin P(m_0)$ we have $\sum_{a\le X,\,a\in\operatorname{orb}(w)}a\ge\Phi(w^\flat)$. Taking logarithmic means as in~\eqref{eq:mean_of_points}, and using~\eqref{eq:reduced} and the end of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} for each $r$, together with $p_0+p_1+p_2=1$, we obtain
\[
\sum_{a\le X}a\,h(a)\ \ge\ M_XX\sum_{r}\delta_{\varphi_r}+\Sigma_X\,h(m_0)\ \ge\ \Bigl(\gamma_0+\frac{h(m_0)}{\rho-1}\Bigr)X-o(X),
\]
which contradicts~\eqref{eq:upper}. Finally, let $c\in\{1,-1\}$. An unbounded orbit contains a number $b=T_{3,c}(m)$ with $m$ odd, the orbit of $b$ is unbounded, and $h(b)>0$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:dichotomy}, because $3\nmid b$. By the first part, this contradicts~\eqref{eq:upper}.
\end{proof}

\section{First-passage tails: reduction, tightness and obstructions}
\label{sec:tail-programme}
%=====================================================================
The lower bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} uses a clipped first-passage
observable. This appendix records what the method gives towards the
matching upper bound~\eqref{eq:upper}, and where it stops. Its results
are conditional on Lemmas~\ref{lem:reduction}, \ref{lem:valuations}
and~\ref{lem:model} with the uniformities used in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}; it does \emph{not} assert that the missing bounds
have been established. For a function $f$ of the odd part $w^\flat$ of
$w$ we write
\[
  \mathbb E_{\log}f(w^\flat)
  :=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log N}
  \sum_{1\le w<N}\frac{f(w^\flat)}{w}
\]
whenever the limit exists. The two open steps are the control of the
clipping tail $T_X$ beyond the coupling horizon
(Remark~\ref{rem:tail-barrier}, Problem~\ref{prob:excursions}) and the
control of the return term $R_X$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:two-tails}). The
intermediate-tail and exceptional-mass arguments in the
\href{https://github.com/Sultanow/collatz/tree/master/07\%20Basins\%20of\%20Attraction/docs/research}{research notes}
are conditional working drafts, not additional certified theorems.

\subsection{Calibration and decomposition}
\label{subsec:clipped-calibration}
Throughout this subsection let $c\ge-1$ be odd with $3\nmid c$, fix
$\beta=1/200$, and set $\gamma_0=2/\log(4/3)$.
For $X>1$ put
\[
 W_X=(X^{1-\beta},X]\cap\N,\qquad X_1=X^{1-2\beta},\qquad
 H_X=(\log X)^{\varepsilon_0/2}X.
\]
For an odd positive integer $m$, let $\Phi_X(m)$ be the sum, counted
with multiplicity, of the elements of $W_X$ in Syracuse blocks
$1,\ldots,\tau_{X_1}(m)$, and put $\Phi_X(m)=0$ if
$\tau_{X_1}(m)=\infty$. For $w\in\N$, let $w^\flat$ be its odd part.
\begin{proposition}[Sharp calibration after clipping]
\label{prop:clipped-calibration}
Assume Lemmas~\ref{lem:reduction}, \ref{lem:valuations}, and
\ref{lem:model} with the uniformities used in the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}. Then the logarithmic mean
\[
 C_X:=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1{\log N}
 \sum_{1\le w<N}\frac{\min\{\Phi_X(w^\flat),H_X\}}{w}
\]
exists for every sufficiently large $X$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:clipped-calibration}
 \boxed{C_X=\gamma_0 X+o(X)\qquad(X\to\infty).}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
For fixed $X$, the first-passage invariance checked in Step~2 of
the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower} holds for
$\varphi_X(m)=\min\{\Phi_X(m),H_X\}/H_X$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction} therefore gives the existence of $C_X$
and, with $x_0=X^{1+\beta}$, $u=x_0^{\lambda_0}$,
$v=u^\alpha$, and $\mathbf N\sim\mathbf{Log}_{u,v}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calib-window}
 C_X=\mathbb E\min\{\Phi_X(\mathbf N),H_X\}
  +O\bigl(H_X(\log u)^{-\varepsilon_0}\bigr)
 =\mathbb E\min\{\Phi_X(\mathbf N),H_X\}+o(X).
\end{equation}
The proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}, Step~3, with the residue
condition removed, shows
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calib-lower}
 \mathbb E\min\{\Phi_X(\mathbf N),H_X\}\ge\gamma_0X-o(X).
\end{equation}
Here one must apply the argument to the \emph{total} block sum;
summing separately clipped residue contributions would not suffice.

We prove the reverse inequality. Couple $\mathbf N$ to
$U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[s,s+L]$ and independent geometric variables
$g_j$ as in Step~3, with $s=\log u$, $L=\log(v/u)$, and
$\varepsilon=X^{-1/2}$. Let $G$ be the good coupling event there;
for some $\eta>0$ it satisfies $\mathbb P(G^c)=O(X^{-\eta})$.
For $j\ge1$ and $0\le i<g_j$, write
$z_{j,i}=U-D_j+i\log2$ as in Lemma~\ref{lem:model}.
The comparison in Step~3 gives uniformly for
$j\le\tau_{X_1}(\mathbf N)$ on $G$,
\[
 \left|\log(2^i\operatorname{Syr}^j(\mathbf N))-z_{j,i}\right|
 \le C\varepsilon,
\]
for all $0\le i<g_j$; in particular the valuations agree up to
this stopping time. (The relative-error formula
\eqref{eq:ideal} uses only preceding ideal iterates, which stay
above $X_1/3$ even for the final block.) Consequently, with
$\ell_-=(1-\beta)\log X-C\varepsilon$,
$\ell_+=\log X+C\varepsilon$, and
\[
 Z^+=\sum_{j\ge1}\sum_{i=0}^{g_j-1}e^{z_{j,i}}
       \mathbf 1[\ell_-<z_{j,i}\le\ell_+],
\]
we have the pointwise upper comparison
\begin{equation}\label{eq:calib-upper-coupling}
 \Phi_X(\mathbf N)\le (1+C\varepsilon)Z^+\quad\text{on }G.
\end{equation}

Let $V(t)$ be the expected number of unmarked model points
$y_{j,i}=D_j-i\log2$ below $t$, over all $j\ge1$ and
$0\le i<g_j$. Summing the three residue classes in the proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:model}, equation~\eqref{eq:V} for $n=1$, gives
$V(t)=\gamma_0t+O(1)$ for $t\ge0$. Thus the same exact
integration identity as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:model}(a)
gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \mathbb EZ^+
 &=\frac1L\int_{\ell_-}^{\ell_+}e^z
             \bigl(V(s+L-z)-V(s-z)\bigr)\,dz\\
 &=\gamma_0\bigl(e^{\ell_+}-e^{\ell_-}\bigr)
       +O\left(\frac{e^{\ell_+}}L\right)
  =\gamma_0 X+o(X),
 \end{aligned}
\]
because $s-\ell_+>0$, $L\asymp\log X$,
$e^{\ell_+}=X(1+O(\varepsilon))$, and
$e^{\ell_-}=O(X^{1-\beta})$.
Since $\min\{\Phi_X,H_X\}\le H_X$ always,
\[
 \mathbb E\min\{\Phi_X(\mathbf N),H_X\}
 \le(1+C\varepsilon)\mathbb EZ^+
     +H_X\mathbb P(G^c)
 =\gamma_0X+o(X).
\]
Together with \eqref{eq:calib-lower} and
\eqref{eq:calib-window}, this proves the claim.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Two nonnegative obstructions to the mean upper bound]
\label{cor:two-tails}
Write $S_X=\sum_{a\le X}a h(a)$, and define
\[
\begin{aligned}
 F_X&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1{\log N}
   \sum_{1\le w<N}\frac{\Phi_X(w^\flat)}w,\\
 R_X&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1{\log N}
   \sum_{1\le w<N}\frac1w
   \left(\sum_{\substack{a\le X\\a\in\operatorname{orb}(w)}}a
   -\Phi_X(w^\flat)\right),
\end{aligned}
\]
where the limits exist by Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction},
Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(a), and finite linearity.
Put
\[
 T_X=F_X-C_X
 =\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1{\log N}
     \sum_{1\le w<N}\frac{(\Phi_X(w^\flat)-H_X)_+}{w}\ge0.
\]
Then
\[
 \boxed{S_X=C_X+T_X+R_X,\qquad T_X\ge0,\quad R_X\ge0.}
\]
In particular, conditional on the stated lemmas,
\[
 \limsup_{X\to\infty}\frac{S_X}{X}\le\gamma_0
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad T_X=o(X)\ \text{and}\ R_X=o(X).
\]
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
For fixed $X$, the first-passage blocks do not repeat points,
by Step~1 of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}. Thus for every $w$,
$0\le\Phi_X(w^\flat)\le
\sum_{a\le X,\ a\in\operatorname{orb}(w)}a$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction} applies to the bounded function
$\Phi_X/B_X$, where $B_X=\sum_{a\in W_X}a$, since the first-passage
invariance of Step~2 holds for all sufficiently large passage
thresholds. Hence $F_X$ exists. Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(a)
and finite linearity show that the logarithmic mean of the full
orbit-point sum is $S_X$. Subtracting proves that $R_X$ exists,
$R_X\ge0$, and $S_X=F_X+R_X$.
Finally $F_X-C_X$ is the mean of $(\Phi_X-H_X)_+$, so $T_X\ge0$. The claimed equivalence follows
from Proposition~\ref{prop:clipped-calibration} and the fact that
both tails are nonnegative.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Moment criteria]
\label{rem:moment-criterion}
For $t\ge0$ and $\theta>0$ we have $(t-H_X)_+\le t^{1+\theta}H_X^{-\theta}$.
Hence, if for some $\theta>0$ and some $M$ independent of large $X$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:weighted-passage-moment}
  \mathbb E_{\log}\Bigl[\Bigl(\frac{\Phi_X(w^\flat)}{X}\Bigr)^{1+\theta}\Bigr]\le M,
\end{equation}
then $T_X\le MX(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0\theta/2}=o(X)$.
Corollary~\ref{cor:extreme-tail}(c) below relaxes
\eqref{eq:weighted-passage-moment} to moments that grow like a small power
of $\log X$. The weighting matters. The unweighted number $V_X(m)$ of
points of $W_X$ in the same blocks satisfies $\Phi_X\le XV_X$, but it
cannot obey a moment bound uniform in $X$: in the model of
Lemma~\ref{lem:model} its mean is $\gamma_0\beta\log X+O(1)$, because
$W_X$ has logarithmic length $\beta\log X$, and the coupling in Step~3 of
the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}, applied with unit weights, transfers
the lower bound $(\gamma_0\beta-o(1))\log X$ to
$\mathbb E_{\log}V_X(w^\flat)$. Most of these points lie far below $X$ and
contribute little to $\Phi_X$. None of these conditions says anything
about the return term $R_X$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Unbounded orbits and the return term]
\label{rem:return-divergence}
If the orbit of some $m_0$ with $h(m_0)>0$ is unbounded, then $\liminf_{X\to\infty}R_X/X\ge2h(m_0)$. Indeed, for $w\in P(m_0)$ the numbers $p_j$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:escape-ladder} lie on the orbit of $w$ but are not counted by $\Phi_X(w^\flat)$, as in the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:remains}(b); hence the summand of $R_X$ at $w$ is at least $\mathbf 1_{P(m_0)}(w)\bigl(X/(\rho-1)-K\bigr)$. Thus $R_X=o(X)$ alone excludes unbounded orbits of positive potential, and for $c=\pm1$ all unbounded orbits.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Exponential tightness of the first-passage mass}
\label{subsec:tightness}
Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction} transfers not only means but also
distribution functions of first-passage observables, with an error that
does not depend on the observable. Combined with the coupling in the
proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:clipped-calibration}, this shows that the
moment condition of Remark~\ref{rem:moment-criterion} can fail only through an exceptional set of
logarithmic density $O((\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0})$. We keep the notation
of Proposition~\ref{prop:clipped-calibration} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:two-tails}, and put, for $\Lambda\ge0$ and $M\ge0$,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \mu_X(\Lambda)&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1{\log N}
   \sum_{1\le w<N}\frac{\mathbf 1[\Phi_X(w^\flat)>\Lambda X]}{w},\\
 T_X(M)&=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1{\log N}
   \sum_{1\le w<N}\frac{(\Phi_X(w^\flat)-MX)_+}{w},
 \end{aligned}
\]
so that $T_X=T_X\bigl((\log X)^{\varepsilon_0/2}\bigr)$.

\begin{proposition}[Exponential tightness up to a logarithmic exceptional set]
\label{prop:tightness}
Assume the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:clipped-calibration}.
There are constants $C\ge1$ and $X_0$, independent of $X$ and $\Lambda$, such that
for all $X\ge X_0$ and all $\Lambda\ge0$ the limit $\mu_X(\Lambda)$
exists and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tightness}
 \mu_X(\Lambda)\le C e^{-\Lambda/C}+C(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1 (transfer).} Fix $X$ and $\Lambda$, and let
$\varphi=\mathbf 1[\Phi_X>\Lambda X]$. By Step~2 of the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}, $\Phi_X(m)=\Phi_X(\operatorname{Pass}_x(m))$ for
all $x\ge x_0=X^{1+\beta}$ and all odd $m>x$ with $\tau_x(m)<\infty$ and
$\operatorname{Pass}_x(m)>\frac34x\exp(-(\log x)^{0.7})$, once $X$ is
large. Every function of $\Phi_X$ inherits this invariance. Hence
Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction} applies to $\varphi$: the limit
$\mu_X(\Lambda)=\delta_\varphi$ exists, and with $u=x_0^{\lambda_0}$,
$v=u^\alpha$ and $\mathbf N\sim\mathbf{Log}_{u,v}$,
\[
 \bigl|\mu_X(\Lambda)-\mathbb P(\Phi_X(\mathbf N)>\Lambda X)\bigr|
 \le C(\log u)^{-\varepsilon_0}\le C(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0}.
\]
The constant of Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction} does not depend on
$\varphi$, hence not on $\Lambda$.

\emph{Step 2 (coupling).} Couple $\mathbf N$, $U$ and $(g_j)$ as in the
proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:clipped-calibration}, with the good event
$G$, $\mathbb P(G^c)\le CX^{-1/C}$, and the upper comparison
\eqref{eq:calib-upper-coupling}. For large $X$ we have
$1+C\varepsilon\le2$, so that
\[
 \mathbb P(\Phi_X(\mathbf N)>\Lambda X)
 \le CX^{-1/C}+\mathbb P(Z^+>\Lambda X/2).
\]

\emph{Step 3 (exponential moments of local counts).} For $q\le s$ let
$N_q$ be the number of pairs $(j,i)$ with $j\ge1$, $0\le i<g_j$ and
$z_{j,i}\in(q-1,q]$. We claim that there are absolute constants
$\theta_0>0$ and $K_0<\infty$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:local-exp-moment}
 \mathbb E\,e^{\theta_0N_q}\le K_0\qquad\text{for all }q\le s.
\end{equation}
As in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:model}(b), let $j_1$ be the first
$j\ge1$ with $U-D_j\le q$, and put $b=1+\log\frac32$; then
\[
 N_q\le g_{j_1}+Y,\qquad
 Y=\sum_{k\ge1}g_{j_1+k}\,\mathbf 1\bigl[D_{j_1+k-1}-D_{j_1}<b\bigr].
\]
Conditionally on $U$, $j_1$ is a stopping time for $(g_j)$. By the strong
Markov property, $(g_{j_1+k})_{k\ge1}$ is an i.i.d.\ sequence with the
law of $g_1$, independent of $U,g_1,\ldots,g_{j_1}$; thus $Y$ is
independent of $g_{j_1}$ and has the law of
$Y_0=\sum_{k\ge1}g_k\mathbf 1[D_{k-1}<b]$.

\emph{The overshooting valuation.} Let $t=U-q\ge0$. The index $j$ is the
first with $D_j\ge t$ and $g_j=v$ only if
$t-v\log2+\log3\le D_{j-1}<t$, an interval of length less than $v$, and
$g_j$ is independent of $D_{j-1}$. By the visit bound in
Lemma~\ref{lem:model}(ii),
\[
 \mathbb P(g_{j_1}=v\mid U)\le 2^{-v}\,
 \mathbb E\#\{j\ge1:\ t-v\log2+\log3\le D_{j-1}<t\}
 \le C(1+v)2^{-v},
\]
so $\mathbb E[e^{\theta g_{j_1}}\mid U]\le C\sum_{v\ge1}(1+v)(e^\theta/2)^v$,
which is bounded uniformly in $U$ for every $\theta<\log2$.

\emph{The returns after the crossing.} Let
$\kappa=1+\sup\{k\ge0:D_k<b\}$, which is finite almost surely. Then
$Y_0\le S_\kappa$ with $S_k=g_1+\dots+g_k$, and by Chernoff's bound in
Lemma~\ref{lem:model}(ii),
$\mathbb P(\kappa\ge k+1)\le\sum_{k'\ge k}e^{b/2}\varrho^{k'}
\le C\varrho^{k}$. Put
$\psi(\theta)=\mathbb E\,e^{\theta g_1}=e^\theta/(2-e^\theta)$. The
Cauchy--Schwarz inequality gives
\[
 \mathbb E\,e^{\theta Y_0}
 \le\sum_{k\ge1}\mathbb E\bigl[e^{\theta S_k}\mathbf 1[\kappa=k]\bigr]
 \le\sum_{k\ge1}\psi(2\theta)^{k/2}\,\mathbb P(\kappa\ge k)^{1/2}
 \le C\sum_{k\ge1}\bigl(\psi(2\theta)\varrho\bigr)^{k/2},
\]
which is finite as soon as $\psi(2\theta)\varrho<1$. Since $\psi(0)=1$
and $\varrho<1$, this holds for all small $\theta>0$. Choosing such a
$\theta_0<\log 2$ and combining the two bounds by independence proves
\eqref{eq:local-exp-moment}.

\emph{Step 4 (conclusion).} Every point counted in $Z^+$ satisfies
$z_{j,i}\le\ell_+\le s$, and $e^{\ell_+}=Xe^{C\varepsilon}\le2X$. Hence
\[
 \frac{Z^+}{X}\le2\sum_{i\ge0}e^{-i}N_{\ell_+-i}
 =\frac{2}{1-e^{-1}}\sum_{i\ge0}w_iN_{\ell_+-i},
 \qquad w_i=(1-e^{-1})e^{-i}.
\]
The weights $w_i$ add up to $1$. With
$\theta_1=\theta_0(1-e^{-1})/2$, convexity of the exponential and
\eqref{eq:local-exp-moment} give
\[
 \mathbb E\exp\bigl(\theta_1Z^+/X\bigr)
 \le\sum_{i\ge0}w_i\,\mathbb E\exp\bigl(\theta_0N_{\ell_+-i}\bigr)\le K_0,
\]
and Markov's inequality gives
$\mathbb P(Z^+>\Lambda X/2)\le K_0e^{-\theta_1\Lambda/2}$.
Together with Steps~1 and~2,
\[
 \mu_X(\Lambda)\le C(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0}+CX^{-1/C}
   +K_0e^{-\theta_1\Lambda/2},
\]
which is \eqref{eq:tightness} after enlarging $C$.
\end{proof}

\noindent
In particular, this yields tightness in probability,
$\lim_{M\to\infty}\limsup_{X\to\infty}\mu_X(M)=0$.
It does \emph{not} yield uniform integrability of $\Phi_X/X$:
the exceptional probability in \eqref{eq:tightness} is independent
of $M$ and can carry a non-negligible weighted contribution.


\begin{corollary}[Where the clipping tail can live]
\label{cor:extreme-tail}
Assume the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:clipped-calibration}, and
let $C$ and $X_0$ be as in Proposition~\ref{prop:tightness}.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
 \item For $X\ge X_0$ and $0\le L\le M$, the means $T_X(L)$ and $T_X(M)$
 exist, and
 \[
  T_X(M)\le T_X(L)\le T_X(M)+C^2Xe^{-L/C}+CXM(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0}.
 \]
 \item If $M_X\ge(\log X)^{\varepsilon_0/2}$ and
 $M_X=o\bigl((\log X)^{\varepsilon_0}\bigr)$, then
 $T_X=T_X(M_X)+o(X)$. In particular, $T_X=o(X)$ if and only if
 $T_X(M_X)=o(X)$.
 \item Suppose that there are $p>1$, $A<(p-1)\varepsilon_0$ and $K$
 such that, for all large $X$,
 \[
  \limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac1{\log N}\sum_{1\le w<N}\frac1w
  \Bigl(\frac{\Phi_X(w^\flat)}X\Bigr)^p\le K(\log X)^A .
 \]
 Then $T_X=O\bigl(X(\log X)^{-\kappa}\bigr)$ with
 $\kappa=\bigl((p-1)\varepsilon_0-A\bigr)/p>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $B_X=\sum_{a\in W_X}a$. By Step~1 of the proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}, $0\le\Phi_X\le B_X$. Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction},
applied to the bounded invariant function $(\Phi_X-MX)_+/B_X$, gives the
existence of $T_X(M)$.

(a) The first inequality holds because $(t-MX)_+\le(t-LX)_+$. For
$t\ge0$,
\[
 (t-LX)_+-(t-MX)_+=X\int_L^M\mathbf 1[t>\lambda X]\,d\lambda .
\]
We insert $t=\Phi_X(w^\flat)$, multiply by $1/w$, sum over $w<N$ and
divide by $\log N$. For each $N$ the finite sum and the integral may be
interchanged. The resulting integrands converge to $\mu_X(\lambda)$ for
every $\lambda$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:tightness}, and they are bounded
by $2$ for large $N$. By dominated convergence and \eqref{eq:tightness},
\[
 T_X(L)-T_X(M)=X\int_L^M\mu_X(\lambda)\,d\lambda
 \le X\int_L^\infty Ce^{-\lambda/C}\,d\lambda+CX(M-L)(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0}.
\]

(b) Apply (a) with $L=(\log X)^{\varepsilon_0/2}$, for which
$T_X(L)=T_X$, and $M=M_X$:
$0\le T_X-T_X(M_X)\le C^2Xe^{-(\log X)^{\varepsilon_0/2}/C}
+CXM_X(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0}=o(X)$.

(c) For $t\ge0$ and $M>0$ we have $(t-MX)_+\le t^p(MX)^{1-p}$, hence
$T_X(M)\le KX(\log X)^AM^{1-p}$. Let $L=(\log X)^{\varepsilon_0/2}$. If
$(A+\varepsilon_0)/p\ge\varepsilon_0/2$, apply (a) with
$M=(\log X)^{(A+\varepsilon_0)/p}\ge L$; then
$KX(\log X)^AM^{1-p}$ and $CXM(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0}$ are both
$O(X(\log X)^{-\kappa})$. Otherwise $A<\varepsilon_0(p/2-1)$, and the
bound with $M=L$ gives directly
$T_X\le KX(\log X)^{A-(p-1)\varepsilon_0/2}$, where
$A-(p-1)\varepsilon_0/2<-\kappa$. In both cases the term
$C^2Xe^{-L/C}$ is $O(X(\log X)^{-\kappa})$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What remains, and what the numerics can see]
\label{rem:tail-barrier}
Proposition~\ref{prop:tightness} says that actual orbits inherit the
exponential tail of the model except on an exceptional set $E_X$ of
logarithmic density $O((\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0})$. The exponent
$\varepsilon_0$ comes from \eqref{eq:ggm_hit} and \eqref{eq:ggm_tv}; it
is small and not explicit. On $E_X$ the only available bound is
$\Phi_X\le B_X<X^2$. Thus the trivial estimate of the contribution of
$E_X$ to $T_X$ is $X^2(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0}$, which misses the
target $o(X)$ by a factor $X(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0}$.
Corollary~\ref{cor:extreme-tail}(b) isolates exactly this: the clipping
tail vanishes if and only if the orbits with $\Phi_X>M_XX$ carry mass
$o(X)$, for one (equivalently, every) choice of $M_X$ between
$(\log X)^{\varepsilon_0/2}$ and $o((\log X)^{\varepsilon_0})$.
By definition, $\Phi_X(m)=0$ if the orbit of $m$ never reaches
$[1,X_1]$. This does not exclude contributions from orbits that
first descend below $X_1$ and later become unbounded or enter a
cycle above $X_1$. The obstruction for the clipping tail consists of
finite initial excursions that, before their first passage below $X_1$,
collect more than $X(\log X)^{\varepsilon_0/2}$ in $W_X$.
Excluding them requires information about individual orbits beyond the
$2$-adic horizon of Lemma~\ref{lem:valuations} and beyond the
total-variation scale of \eqref{eq:ggm_tv}; return certificates that
depend only on a residue modulo a fixed power of $2$ cannot provide it
(Proposition~\ref{prop:residue-obstruction}).

Numerically, the typical part behaves exactly as in the model (Run~I).
The program \path{tail_check.c} draws odd starts logarithmically from the
window $[X^{1+\beta},X^{(1+\beta)^2}]$ used above and computes
$\Phi_X/X$ along the actual orbit and along the random walk of
Lemma~\ref{lem:model}; starts whose orbits leave $128$-bit arithmetic
are recomputed exactly. For $c\in\{1,-1,5\}$,
$X\in\{10^{12},10^{20},10^{30}\}$ and $\beta\in\{0.05,0.005\}$, with
$4\cdot10^6$ starts per configuration ($2\cdot10^7$ for $c=1$,
$X=10^{30}$, $\beta=0.05$), the means and second moments of $\Phi_X/X$
agree with the model within the sampling error. For example, for $c=1$,
$X=10^{30}$ and $\beta=0.05$ the mean is $6.714$ against $6.712$ and the
second moment $69.33$ against $69.27$. The tail probabilities
$\mathbb P(\Phi_X/X>\Lambda)$ agree in all $18$ configurations with
$|z|\le2.1$. The sampled tails are consistent with exponential decay, with an
empirically fitted rate of about $0.21$ for $\beta=0.05$ and
$X\ge10^{20}$; this finite-sample fit is not an asymptotic tail
estimate. The largest recorded value among the $2\cdot10^7$ starts
is $\Phi_X/X=87.9$. Such samples see sets of
density down to about $10^{-7}$. A set that could spoil $T_X=o(X)$ needs
density only about $X^{-1}$, so the numerics support the model but do
not test the exceptional set.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Return certificates and their limits}
\label{subsec:lyapunov-return}
Finite parity-cylinder balance holds up to any fixed horizon, but it
cannot be restarted after many returns, and certificates built on a fixed
residue fail on all large shells. The following results make this
precise.

\begin{lemma}[Finite-horizon residue balance; cf. Terras~\cite{Terras_1976}]
\label{lem:finite-horizon-residue}
Fix an odd $c\ge-1$, a positive stopping threshold, a shell $I$ and a
starting interval $J=[A,D)\cap\N$ with $A\ge1$. Let $E_{k,\le T}$ be
the starts $m\in J$ whose $k$-th visit to $I$ occurs by time $T$ before stopping,
and let $Y_k$ be that visited state. For any $b\ge1$ and any set
$G\subset\Z/2^b\Z$, put $p=|G|/2^b$. Then
\[
\left|\sum_{m\in E_{k,\le T}}\frac{\mathbf1_G(Y_k(m))-p}{m}\right|
\le\frac{8(T+1)2^{T+b}}{A}.
\]
The same bound without $1/A$ holds for unweighted counting.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix a time $t\le T$ and a parity cylinder $m=r+2^t j$.
For $0\le s\le t$, the iterate is affine and increasing in $j$,
with $T_{3,c}^t(m)$ advancing by the odd step $3^{h_t}$, where $h_t$ is
the number of odd steps among the first $t$, which is fixed on the cylinder.
Thus its values cycle through all classes modulo $2^b$.
Stopping and shell-visit conditions partition the $j$-range into at
most $3(t+1)+1$ intervals. Each contributes discrepancy at most
$2^b$, also after harmonic weighting by summation by parts (with an
additional factor $1/A$). Sum over $2^t$ cylinders and $0\le t\le T$.
\end{proof}
This is a quantitative form of classical parity-cylinder balance,
not a new long-time mixing theorem. Its error is absolute, not
relative to the mass of $E_{k,\le T}$, and it gives no uniform bound
for visits beyond the chosen horizon.

\begin{remark}[Finite-support obstruction to independent restarts]
\label{rem:entrance-support}
Let $I_{X,j}$ be one of the finite dyadic shells, and suppose the
conditional distribution $\mu_{X,j,k}$ of the odd part of its $k$-th
visited state is defined. It is supported on at most
$s_{X,j}=\#(I_{X,j}\cap\mathbb N)$ states. Writing $U_B$ for the
uniform law on the $2^{B-1}$ odd classes modulo $2^B$ (with $d_{\mathrm{TV}}$ defined as the supremum over events), we therefore have
\[
 d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mu_{X,j,k}\bmod 2^B,U_B)
 \ge 1-\frac{s_{X,j}}{2^{B-1}}.
\]
Indeed, the union of residue classes in the support has
$\mu_{X,j,k}$-mass one and uniform mass at most
$s_{X,j}/2^{B-1}$. For fixed $X,j$, the right-hand side tends to one
as $B\to\infty$. Thus the usual approximate uniformity modulo large
powers of two cannot simply be restarted after arbitrarily many
shell returns. This observation does \emph{not} disprove geometric
return tails. It identifies a limitation of the direct independent-
valuation transfer and motivates a conditional entrance-measure
criterion, developed separately in the research notes. Neither this
remark nor the finite Lean modules proves the open return-moment
bound or $T_X=o(X)$.
\end{remark}

The Green-operator identities for finite return chains can be used without
assuming a strict contraction of the ordinary occupation measure.  Here is
an alternative that targets the second moment directly.
Fix a finite shell $I\subset\N$ and a first-passage threshold $a<\min I$.
Restrict attention to states $y\in I$ whose forward orbit under $T_{3,c}$
reaches a value below $a$.  Let $R_I(y)$ be the next visit to $I$
\emph{before} that passage, or $\dagger$ if there is none.  On the
resulting terminating return graph write
\[
  (P_I F)(y)=
  \begin{cases}F(R_I(y)),&R_I(y)\ne\dagger,\\
  0,&R_I(y)=\dagger.
  \end{cases}
\]
Let $N_I(y)\ge1$ denote the number of visits from $y$, including the
initial visit.  This definition excludes unresolved orbits; no assertion
about their convergence is implicit.

\begin{proposition}[Two-level return certificate]
\label{prop:lyapunov-return}
Suppose nonnegative functions $U,V$ on the terminating return graph obey
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-level-lyapunov}
 U\ge1+P_IU,\qquad V\ge1+2P_IU+P_IV.
\end{equation}
Then $N_I(y)\le U(y)$ and $N_I(y)^2\le V(y)$ for every state $y$ of
that graph.  Consequently, for any nonnegative finite first-entry
subprobability $q_1$ supported on these states,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lyapunov-second-moment}
 \sum_{y\in I}q_1(y)N_I(y)^2
 \le \sum_{y\in I}q_1(y)V(y).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Induct backwards along the finite return chain.  At a terminal state,
$P_IU=P_IV=0$ and $N_I=1$, so both inequalities follow.  If the next
return is $z$ and $N_I(z)=n$, then $N_I(y)=n+1$.  The induction
hypotheses and~\eqref{eq:two-level-lyapunov} give
\[
 U(y)\ge1+U(z)\ge n+1,\qquad
 V(y)\ge1+2U(z)+V(z)\ge1+2n+n^2=(n+1)^2.
\]
Multiply the latter inequality by $q_1(y)$ and sum.
\end{proof}

The functions $U(y)=N_I(y)$ and $V(y)=N_I(y)^2$ themselves satisfy
\eqref{eq:two-level-lyapunov} with equality.  Thus existence of a
certificate on a fixed finite graph is automatic.  The real problem is
to find \emph{explicit certificates with uniformly bounded first-entry
averages} as the shell varies.  In the dyadic decomposition
$I_{X,j}=(X2^{-j-1},X2^{-j}]\cap W_X$ of the observable $\Phi_X$, write
$q_{1,X,j}$ for the logarithmically induced (possibly subprobability)
first-entry law and $N_{X,j}$ for the corresponding number of visits.

\begin{corollary}[Conditional removal of the clipping tail]
\label{cor:lyapunov-tail}
Assume the first-entry laws and corresponding second moments exist,
and, for all sufficiently large $X$ and every relevant $j$, that
functions $U_{X,j},V_{X,j}$ satisfy~\eqref{eq:two-level-lyapunov} on
the supported terminating return paths with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lyapunov-uniform-open}
 \sup_{X,j}\sum_{y\in I_{X,j}}q_{1,X,j}(y)V_{X,j}(y)
 \le B<\infty.
\end{equation}
Then $T_X\le 4BX^2/H_X=o(X)$, where $T_X,H_X$ are defined in
Corollary~\ref{cor:two-tails}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:lyapunov-return}, every shell count has second
moment at most $B$.  Each point in $I_{X,j}$ has weight at most
$X2^{-j}$, so Minkowski's inequality gives
\[
 \left\|\frac{\Phi_X}{X}\right\|_2
 \le\sum_{j\ge0}2^{-j}\|N_{X,j}\|_2\le2\sqrt B.
\]
For $t\ge0$ one has $(t-H_X)_+\le t^2/H_X$.  Taking the logarithmic
mean yields $T_X\le\mathbb E_{\log}(\Phi_X^2)/H_X\le4BX^2/H_X$.
Since $H_X=X(\log X)^{\varepsilon_0/2}$, this is $o(X)$.
\end{proof}

There is a simple arithmetic reason why certificates based solely on a
fixed binary residue cannot work on all large dyadic shells.

\begin{proposition}[Fixed-modulus obstruction]
\label{prop:residue-obstruction}
For every odd $c\in\Z$ and every $k\ge0$, put $X_0(c,k)=24\cdot2^k+12|c|+24$.
For every real $X\ge X_0(c,k)$ one can find positive integers
$y,z\in(X/2,X]$ with
\[
 T_{3,c}(y)=z,\qquad y\equiv z\pmod{2^k}.
\]
Consequently, on any such shell, no function $U$ depending only on
$y\bmod2^k$ can satisfy $U\ge1+P_IU$ at every state of a return graph
containing this one-step return (with any cutoff $a<X/2$).
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Set $m=2^k$.  For $X\ge X_0(c,k)$, the interval
\[
 \left(\frac X4+\frac c2,\;\frac{X+c}{3}\right]
\]
has length $X/12-c/6>m$ and is contained in $(0,\infty)$.
It therefore contains a positive multiple $A$ of $m$.  Set
$y=2A-c$ and $z=3A-c$.  Since $c$ is odd, $y$ is odd and
$T_{3,c}(y)=(3y+c)/2=z$; the inequalities for $A$ imply
$X/2<y<z\le X$.  Finally $z-y=A\equiv0\pmod m$.
The next shell return from $y$ is thus $z$ itself.  A residue-only
$U$ would give $U(y)=U(z)$ while the first inequality in
\eqref{eq:two-level-lyapunov} forces $U(y)\ge1+U(z)$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Lean verification and the unresolved step]
\label{rem:lyapunov-certification}
The \href{https://github.com/Sultanow/collatz/blob/ee5a5b4/07\%20Basins\%20of\%20Attraction/lean/CollatzBasins/ReturnLyapunov.lean}{Lean~4 return-certificate module}
formalises the finite rational-valued certificate and weighted inequality,
as well as exact one-step arithmetic and an explicit family of dyadic
congruence witnesses.  The module \href{https://github.com/Sultanow/collatz/blob/ee5a5b4/07\%20Basins\%20of\%20Attraction/lean/CollatzBasins/ReturnLargeShell.lean}{\texttt{ReturnLargeShell.lean}}
formalises Proposition~\ref{prop:residue-obstruction} for every real
$X\ge X_0(c,k)$; it does not assert that the exhibited return lies on an
orbit that terminates below a given cutoff.  The related finite
\href{https://github.com/Sultanow/collatz/blob/ee5a5b4/07\%20Basins\%20of\%20Attraction/lean/CollatzBasins/ReturnGreen.lean}{Green identities}
and \href{https://github.com/Sultanow/collatz/blob/ee5a5b4/07\%20Basins\%20of\%20Attraction/lean/CollatzBasins/EntranceTransport.lean}{entrance-measure inequalities}
are also formalised.  None of these kernel checks establishes
\eqref{eq:lyapunov-uniform-open} for the actual logarithmic entrance
measures, nor the bounds $T_X=o(X)$ or $R_X=o(X)$ without further
assumptions.  Certificates must encode more than a fixed congruence
class, for instance relative shell position or return-history data.
\end{remark}

The open part can be localised further. Order the points counted by $\Phi_X(m)$ as they occur along the orbit. For $L\ge0$ and $\Phi_X(m)>LX$ let $J_{X,L}(m)\in W_X$ be the point at which their partial sums first exceed $LX$, and put
\[
 \eta_{X,L}(y)=\mathbb E_{\log}\mathbf 1\bigl[J_{X,L}(w^\flat)=y\bigr]\qquad(y\in W_X),
\]
the \emph{first-excess entrance law}; it exists by Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction}, as $\mu_X(\Lambda)$ does, and its total mass is $\mu_X(L)$. For $y\in W_X$ let $r_X(y)$ be $X^{-1}$ times the sum of the later points of $W_X$ on the orbit of $y$ before its first value at most $X_1$, and let $N^+_{X,j}(y)$ be the number of those in $I_{X,j}$. (Counting along $T_{3,c}$ instead of Syracuse blocks gives the same points: the members of the final block below $X_1$ lie outside $W_X$.)

\begin{proposition}[First-excess transport]
\label{prop:first-excess}
Assume the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:clipped-calibration}, and let $L\ge0$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
 \item $T_X(L)/X=\sum_{y\in W_X}\eta_{X,L}(y)\,r_X(y)+\vartheta$ with $0\le\vartheta\le\mu_X(L)$.
 \item For all integers $K_j\ge0$, with $\mathcal E_X(L;K)=\sum_{y}\eta_{X,L}(y)\sum_{j\ge0}2^{-j}\bigl(N^+_{X,j}(y)-K_j\bigr)_+$,
 \[
  \tfrac12\,\mathcal E_X(L;K)\ \le\ \frac{T_X(L)}X\ \le\ \mu_X(L)\Bigl(1+\sum_{j\ge0}2^{-j}K_j\Bigr)+\mathcal E_X(L;K).
 \]
 \item Let $0<\sigma<\varepsilon_0$, $0\le\omega<1$, $L_X=(\log X)^{\varepsilon_0/2}$ and $K_{X,j}=\lceil(\log X)^\sigma2^{\omega j}\rceil$. Then $T_X=o(X)$ if and only if $\mathcal E_X(L_X;K_X)\to0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) If $\Phi_X(m)>LX$, the excess $(\Phi_X(m)-LX)/X$ is the overshoot at the first crossing, which lies in $(0,1]$ because every point is at most $X$, plus the mass of the later points. The orbit after the point $J_{X,L}(m)$ is determined by this point, so the later mass is $r_X(J_{X,L}(m))$. Taking logarithmic means gives (a). (b) For $z\in I_{X,j}$ we have $2^{-j-1}<z/X\le2^{-j}$, hence $\frac12\sum_j2^{-j}N^+_{X,j}(y)\le r_X(y)\le\sum_j2^{-j}N^+_{X,j}(y)$, and $N\le K+(N-K)_+$. (c) By Proposition~\ref{prop:tightness}, $\mu_X(L_X)=O((\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0})$, while $\sum_j2^{-j}K_{X,j}=O((\log X)^\sigma)$; so the first term in (b) is $o(1)$, and $T_X(L_X)=T_X$.
\end{proof}

In kernel form, $\sum_y\eta_{X,L}(y)\mathbf 1[N^+_{X,j}(y)\ge n]=\eta_{X,L}E_{X,j}Q_{X,j}^{\,n-1}\mathbf 1$, where $E_{X,j}$ is the first-entry kernel into $I_{X,j}$ and $Q_{X,j}$ the first-return kernel on $I_{X,j}$ of the terminating return graph of Proposition~\ref{prop:lyapunov-return}. The first $(\log X)^\sigma2^{\omega j}$ later visits to each shell are thus free; only an excess of late returns of a rare entrance law matters.

\begin{problem}[Weighted occupation of long finite excursions]
\label{prob:excursions}
Show that unusually long but finite first-passage excursions carry a weighted occupation mass $o(X)$ in $W_X$, that is, $\mathcal E_X(L_X;K_X)\to0$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:first-excess}(c); this is equivalent to $T_X=o(X)$. The object is the first-excess entrance law $\eta_{X,L_X}$, of total mass $O((\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0})$, transported by the return kernels of the shells $I_{X,j}$; the residues of its first steps cannot carry the argument (Remark~\ref{rem:entrance-support}, Proposition~\ref{prop:residue-obstruction}). It suffices to control the survival $s_{X,j}(n)=\eta_{X,L_X}E_{X,j}Q_{X,j}^{\,n-1}\mathbf 1$ at the dyadic return counts $n=2^\ell K_{X,j}$, summed over the shells. Put
\[
 B_{X,\ell}=\sum_{j\ge0}2^{-j}K_{X,j}\,s_{X,j}\bigl(2^\ell K_{X,j}\bigr),\qquad D_X=\sum_{\ell\ge1}2^\ell B_{X,\ell},\qquad S_X=\sum_{j\ge0}2^{-j}K_{X,j}.
\]
Since $s_{X,j}$ is nonincreasing, grouping the counts $n\in(2^\ell K_{X,j},2^{\ell+1}K_{X,j}]$ gives $\frac12D_X\le\mathcal E_X(L_X;K_X)\le B_{X,0}+D_X$, and $B_{X,0}\le\mu_X(L_X)S_X$. Hence $T_X=o(X)$ if and only if $D_X\to0$, and an aggregate polynomial survival bound
\[
 B_{X,\ell}\ \le\ A\,2^{-(1+\kappa)\ell}\,\mu_X(L_X)\,S_X\qquad(\ell\ge1),
\]
with constants $A$ and $\kappa>0$, gives $T_X=O\bigl(X(\log X)^{\sigma-\varepsilon_0}\bigr)$. It holds if $B_{X,\ell+1}\le qB_{X,\ell}$ for all $\ell$ with a fixed $q<\frac12$, and if every shell satisfies $s_{X,j}(n)\le A\,\mu_X(L_X)(K_{X,j}/n)^{1+\kappa}$ for $n>K_{X,j}$, but it does not need the latter shell by shell. An additive error $e_{X,\ell}$ in the ratio condition is admissible if $\sum_\ell2^\ell e_{X,\ell}\to0$; an error of the size $(\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0}$ of the exceptional set is not (Remark~\ref{rem:tail-barrier}).
\end{problem}

\begin{remark}[Arithmetic rigidity of returns]
\label{rem:rigidity}
Let $h_t(z)$ be the number of odd values among $z,T_{3,c}(z),\dots,T_{3,c}^{t-1}(z)$, and $\zeta=\log2/\log3\approx0.631$. Multiplying the steps of $T_{3,c}$ gives the exact identity
\[
 \log\frac{T_{3,c}^t(z)}{z}=h_t(z)\log3-t\log2+\sum_{\substack{0\le i<t\\ T_{3,c}^i(z)\ \mathrm{odd}}}\log\Bigl(1+\frac{c}{3\,T_{3,c}^i(z)}\Bigr),
\]
whose last sum is $O(|c|t/X_1)$ while the orbit stays above $X_1$. Hence a return of $z\in I_{X,j}$ to $I_{X,j}$ after $t$ steps forces $|h_t(z)-\zeta t|\le\zeta+O(|c|t/X_1)$, at every time $t$, and survival above $X_1$ for $t$ steps from $W_X$ forces $h_t(z)\ge\zeta t-2\beta\log X/\log3-O(|c|t/X_1)$. Fix $\zeta_0\in(\frac12,\zeta)$, and put
\[
 \Delta_0=\zeta_0\log(2\zeta_0)+(1-\zeta_0)\log(2-2\zeta_0)>0,\qquad t_X=\Bigl\lceil\frac{3\beta\log X}{(\zeta-\zeta_0)\log3}\Bigr\rceil.
\]
The first $t$ parities run bijectively through the residue classes modulo $2^t$ (Terras \cite{Terras_1976}; the induction works for every odd $c$), and at most $2^te^{-t\Delta_0}$ parity words of length $t$ have at least $\zeta_0t$ odd entries. Let $\nu$ be a finite measure on $I_{X,j}$ that gives every class modulo $2^t$ at most $A\,2^{-t}|\nu|$. Then, for large $X$ and $t\ge t_0(\zeta_0)$, the states that return to $I_{X,j}$ at time $t$ without passing below $X_1$ carry $\nu$-mass at most $Ae^{-t\Delta_0}|\nu|$; for $t\ge t_X$ the same bound holds for survival above $X_1$ during $t$ steps.

Since a class modulo $2^{t_X}$ lies in one class modulo $2^t$ for every $t\le t_X$, it suffices to look at the single modulus $2^{t_X}=X^{O(\beta)}$.
\end{remark}

For $A\ge1$ and a finite measure $\nu$ on $\N$ let
\[
 \mathfrak d_t(\nu;A)=\sum_{r\bmod2^t}\bigl(\nu(r+2^t\Z)-A\,2^{-t}|\nu|\bigr)_+
\]
be the mass by which $\nu$ exceeds the relative cap $A\,2^{-t}|\nu|$ on the classes modulo $2^t$. Let $\nu_{X,j,n}=\eta_{X,L_X}E_{X,j}Q_{X,j}^{\,n-1}$ be the law of the $n$-th later visit to $I_{X,j}$, so that $|\nu_{X,j,n}|=s_{X,j}(n)$.

\begin{proposition}[Single-depth concentration criterion]
\label{prop:single-depth}
Assume the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:first-excess}(c). Let $A>1$, choose an integer $b\ge t_0(\zeta_0)$ with $q=Ae^{-\Delta_0b}/(1-e^{-\Delta_0})<1$, and put
\[
 \mathfrak D_X(A)=\sum_{j\ge0}2^{-j}\sum_{n>K_{X,j}}\mathfrak d_{t_X}(\nu_{X,j,n};A).
\]
Then, for all large $X$,
\[
 \mathfrak D_X(A)\ \le\ \mathcal E_X(L_X;K_X)\ \le\ \frac{2b\,\mu_X(L_X)+\mathfrak D_X(A)}{1-q}.
\]
In particular, $T_X=o(X)$ if and only if $\mathfrak D_X(A)\to0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The identity $(N-K)_+=\sum_{n>K}\mathbf 1[N\ge n]$ gives
\[
 \mathcal E_X(L_X;K_X)=\sum_{j\ge0}2^{-j}\sum_{n>K_{X,j}}s_{X,j}(n),
\]
and $\mathfrak d_t(\nu;A)\le|\nu|$; this is the lower bound. For the upper bound, let $y$ be a state at the $n$-th visit that makes $b$ further visits, the last one at time $\tau\ge b$. If $\tau<t_X$, Remark~\ref{rem:rigidity} gives $h_\tau(y)\ge\zeta_0\tau$; otherwise $y$ survives $t_X$ steps, and $h_{t_X}(y)\ge\zeta_0t_X$. In both cases the parity word of length $t_X$ of $y$ has a prefix of some length $t\in[b,t_X]$ with at least $\zeta_0t$ odd entries. By Terras' bijection these words form a set $\mathcal C_X$ of classes modulo $2^{t_X}$ of density $\alpha_X\le\sum_{t\ge b}e^{-\Delta_0t}=q/A$. Each class carries at most $A\,2^{-t_X}|\nu|$ plus its excess, so
\[
 s_{X,j}(n+b)\le\nu_{X,j,n}(\mathcal C_X)\le q\,s_{X,j}(n)+\mathfrak d_{t_X}(\nu_{X,j,n};A).
\]
Summing over $n>K$ and using $s_{X,j}\le\mu_X(L_X)$ for the first $b$ terms gives $(1-q)\sum_{n>K}s_{X,j}(n)\le b\,\mu_X(L_X)+\sum_{n>K}\mathfrak d_{t_X}(\nu_{X,j,n};A)$. Take $K=K_{X,j}$ and sum over $j$ with the weights $2^{-j}$. The last claim follows from Propositions~\ref{prop:tightness} and~\ref{prop:first-excess}(c).
\end{proof}

The criterion is an equivalence. It does not make Problem~\ref{prob:excursions} easier, but it restates it as a question about the $2$-adic distribution of the return laws at the single modulus $2^{t_X}$. The same two-sided bound holds, with $\alpha_X$ in place of $q$, for the excess $(\nu(\mathcal C_X)-\alpha_X|\nu|)_+$ on the dangerous classes alone. The defect has two sources, and neither is reached by the parity counting of Remark~\ref{rem:rigidity} and Lemma~\ref{lem:finite-horizon-residue}; this is where new mathematics is needed. First, the return laws are conditioned on repeated survival and have total mass $O((\log X)^{-\varepsilon_0})$, so they may concentrate in relative terms, while Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction} bounds only absolute errors of that size (Remark~\ref{rem:tail-barrier}). Second, a law $\nu$ on $r$ states has $\mathfrak d_t(\nu;A)\ge(1-Ar\,2^{-t})_+|\nu|$ (Remark~\ref{rem:entrance-support}). The laws of the latest visits live on few states, so once $r\le2^{t_X-1}/A$ they contribute at least half their mass; their contribution can only be made small by bounding the later visits of these few orbits directly. The converse is false: a law on many states can still have a large defect through a few heavy atoms.

The potential gives a criterion of a different kind. Fix $\zeta_0=0.55$ in Remark~\ref{rem:rigidity}; then $2^{t_X}\le2X^{0.12}$. For an integer $b\le t_X$ let $\mathcal C_{X,b}$ be the set $\mathcal C_X$ of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:single-depth} formed with this $b$, a union of classes modulo $2^{t_X}$ of density at most $\alpha_b=e^{-\Delta_0b}/(1-e^{-\Delta_0})$. Let $h^\downarrow(y)=h(y)$ if the orbit of $y$ reaches $[1,X_1]$, and $h^\downarrow(y)=0$ otherwise.

\begin{proposition}[Domination by the stopped potential of odd targets]
\label{prop:potential-domination}
Assume the hypotheses of Proposition~\ref{prop:first-excess}(c). There is $b_0=b_0(\zeta_0)$ such that, for all large $X$ and every integer $b\in[b_0,t_X]$,
\[
 \frac{T_X}X\ \le\ (3+2b)\,\mu_X(L_X)+\frac8X\sum_{\substack{X_1<a\le X,\ a\text{ odd}\\ a\in\mathcal C_{X,b}}}a\,h^\downarrow(a).
\]
Consequently, $T_X=o(X)$ if the stopped profile of the odd targets is uniformly integrable on average:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:UI}
 \lim_{M\to\infty}\ \limsup_{X\to\infty}\ \frac1X\sum_{\substack{X_1<a\le X,\ a\text{ odd}\\ a\,h^\downarrow(a)>M}}a\,h^\downarrow(a)=0.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix a shell $I_{X,j}$, put $U=X2^{-j}$ and $J=((U-c)/3,U]$, and follow the orbit of a point $y\in W_X$ after $y$ and before its first value at most $X_1$. A halving step cannot enter $(U/2,U]$ from below, and an odd step that enters it from below starts in $((U-c)/3,U/2]$. An entry from above ends an excursion above $U$, which starts with the first point or with an odd step from $((2U-c)/3,U]$. These odd points of $J$ are distinct, so the orbit visits $I_{X,j}$ at most $1+O_j(y)$ times, where $O_j(y)$ is the number of its odd points in $J$, and $r_X(y)\le\sum_j2^{-j}\bigl(1+O_j(y)\bigr)$. The endpoints of $J$ have ratio less than $4$. As in Remark~\ref{rem:rigidity}, a return of an odd point of $J$ to $J$ after $t\ge b_0$ steps, and survival above $X_1$ for $t\ge t_X$ steps, force at least $\zeta_0t$ odd entries among the first $t$ parities. Hence every odd point of $J$ that is followed by $b$ further odd points of $J$ lies in $\mathcal C_{X,b}$, and $O_j(y)$ is at most $b$ plus the number of odd $a\in J\cap\mathcal C_{X,b}$ on the later orbit of $y$. A terminating orbit visits no point twice, and a starting value whose orbit visits $a$ lies in the predecessor set of $a$ (compare Step~1 of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:lower}). So the $\eta_{X,L_X}$-mass of the points $y$ whose later orbit visits $a$ is at most $h^\downarrow(a)$. With Proposition~\ref{prop:first-excess}(a), this gives
\[
 \frac{T_X}X\ \le\ \mu_X(L_X)+2(1+b)\,\mu_X(L_X)+\sum_j2^{-j}\sum_{\substack{a\in J\cap\mathcal C_{X,b}\\ a\text{ odd}}}h^\downarrow(a).
\]
For large $X$ every band $J$ lies in $(X_1,X]$, every $a$ lies in at most two of them, and $2^{-j}=U/X<(3a+c)/X\le4a/X$ for $a\in J$. For the consequence, take $b=\lceil(\log\log X)^2\rceil$. Then $b\,\mu_X(L_X)\to0$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:tightness}, at most $(\alpha_b+2^{t_X}/X)X=o(X)$ integers up to $X$ lie in $\mathcal C_{X,b}$, and splitting the sum at $a\,h^\downarrow(a)=M$ bounds it by $M\cdot o(1)$ plus the tail in~\eqref{eq:UI}.
\end{proof}

Only odd targets enter. For odd $a$ with $y=T_{3,c}(a)$ not periodic, Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(b) gives $h(a)=h(y)-h(2y)$. So~\eqref{eq:UI} concerns only the dyadic differences of the potential, and long doubling chains, on which $y\,h(y)$ may be large without any effect on $T_X$, do not enter. Conversely, the same identity writes $h(y)$ as $h(2^Ky)$ plus the sum of $h$ over the odd predecessors of $y,2y,\dots,2^{K-1}y$, so for the actual potential the restriction changes little (Run~K below). Unlike Proposition~\ref{prop:single-depth}, the hypothesis~\eqref{eq:UI} concerns the unconditioned potential, not the conditioned return laws, and it is sufficient, not necessary. It implies a bounded mean of the stopped profile of the odd targets, a weak form of~\eqref{eq:upper} for the targets whose orbits come down. Unlike~\eqref{eq:upper}, it says nothing about unbounded orbits, since all their points have $h^\downarrow=0$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:remains}(b)). It is what Conjecture~\ref{conj:profile} suggests for individual targets. By \cite[(1.26)]{Tao_2022}, the densities $3^n\,\mathbb P(c\cdot\mathbf{Syrac}(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)=\cdot)$ converge in $L^1(\mathbb Z_3)$ as $n\to\infty$, so the Syracuse profile itself is uniformly integrable. A bound $\sup_X X^{-1}\sum_{X_1<a\le X,\,a\text{ odd}}(a\,h^\downarrow(a))^p<\infty$ for some $p>1$ implies~\eqref{eq:UI}. Whether~\eqref{eq:UI} holds is open.

\paragraph{Run~K.} We recomputed the predecessor counts of Run~G with $N=3\cdot10^{10}$ for all targets $a\le2^{22}$ of $3x+1$, $3x-1$, $3x+5$ and $3x+13$, estimated $h(a)$ by the proportion of the starting values, and evaluated $v(a)=a\,h(a)$ on the windows $(X/2,X]$. The counts satisfy the identity of Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(b) exactly, and every target lies on an orbit that reaches $[1,X^{1-2\beta}]$, so here $h^\downarrow=h$. The distribution of $v$ is the same for the four maps and stable from $X=2^{14}$ to $2^{22}$ (Table~\ref{table:ui}, Figure~\ref{fig:ui}). At $X=2^{22}$ its median is $2.3$, its $99$th percentile $68$, and its mean lies between $6.91$ and $6.98$, against $2/\log(4/3)=6.952$. The distribution is heavy-tailed. Targets with $v(a)>20$ carry half of the mean, those with $v(a)>100$ about $15\,\%$, and those with $v(a)>1000$ between $1.4$ and $2.4\,\%$. Restricted to odd targets, as in~\eqref{eq:UI}, these shares change by at most $0.4$ percentage points (rows `odd' of Table~\ref{table:ui}). For $20\le M\le200$ the share above $M$ decays like $M^{-\gamma}$, with fitted exponents $\gamma\approx0.8$. The Syracuse profile $v_n(a)=\frac{2}{\log(4/3)}\,3^n\,\mathbb P(c\cdot\mathbf{Syrac}(\mathbb Z/3^n\mathbb Z)=a)$, computed exactly, has the same tail. Its far tail grows with $n$, as the measured one grows with $X$, and it converges by \cite[(1.26)]{Tao_2022}. The data are therefore consistent with~\eqref{eq:UI}, but they cannot decide the uniformity in $X$, and an $L^p$ bound can hold at most for $p<1+\gamma$. Finally, the mass of $v$ on the dangerous classes of depth $12$ ($b=8$, $\zeta_0=0.55$) equals their density to within $2\,\%$ for $X\ge2^{18}$, and for the odd targets at $X=2^{22}$ to within $0.4\,\%$ (last column of Table~\ref{table:ui}). The potential does not concentrate on these classes, and the sum in Proposition~\ref{prop:potential-domination} is of the order of $\alpha_b$ times the mean.

\begin{lemma}[One block, two laws]
\label{lem:two-laws}
Assume the conclusion of \cite[Section~6]{Allikvere_2026}. There is $c'>0$ such that for all sufficiently large $x$ and $y\in\{x^\alpha,x^{\alpha^2}\}$
\begin{align}
 \mathbb P\bigl(\tau_x(\mathbf U_y)=\infty\bigr)&\ll x^{-c'},\label{eq:two-laws-hit}\\
 \sup_{E}\bigl|\mathbb P\bigl(\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf U_y)\in E\bigr)-\mathbb P\bigl(\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf L_y)\in E\bigr)\bigr|&\ll(\log x)^{-c'},\label{eq:two-laws-tv}
\end{align}
where $E$ runs over the sets of odd integers in $[1,x]$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By the transfer, \eqref{eq:two-laws-hit} is \cite[(D.1)]{Allikvere_2026} for $T_{3,c}$. For a set $E$ of odd integers in $[1,x]$ and $m\le m_0$ put
\[
 S_m(E)=\sum_{M\in E'(E)}\frac{3^m\,p_m(M\bmod 3^m)}{M},
\]
and let $E_x$ be the set of all odd integers in $[1,x]$. By \cite[Section~6.4]{Allikvere_2026} for $T_{3,c}$,
\[
 \mathbb P\bigl(\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf U_y)\in E\bigr)=\bigl(1+O(\log^{-c'}x)\bigr)\frac{S_{m_1}(E)}{S_{m_1}(E_x)}+O(\log^{-c'}x),
\]
with $S_{m_1}(E_x)\asymp1$; the normaliser comes from $E=E_x$, for which the probability is $1$ by the convention $\operatorname{Pass}_x=1$ on no hit. By \cite[(5.7), (5.20), (5.21)]{Tao_2022} for $T_{3,c}$, normalised in the same way, the same holds for $\mathbf L_y$ with $m_0$ in place of $m_1$. It remains to compare $S_{m_0}$ and $S_{m_1}$. For $N\in\mathbb Z/3^{m_0}\mathbb Z$ put $C(N)=\sum_{M\in E',\,M\equiv N\ (3^{m_0})}3^{m_0}/M$. The argument of \cite[Step~3 in the proof of Proposition~3.5]{GGM_2025}, with $n=2m_0$, gives $C(N)\ll1$ uniformly in $N$. Hence, uniformly in $E$,
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \bigl|S_{m_0}(E)-S_{m_1}(E)\bigr|
 &\le\sum_{N\in\mathbb Z/3^{m_0}\mathbb Z}\Bigl|p_{m_0}(N)-3^{m_1-m_0}p_{m_1}(N\bmod3^{m_1})\Bigr|\,C(N)\\
 &\ll\operatorname{Osc}_{m_1,m_0}\bigl(p_{m_0}(N)\bigr)_N\ll_Am_1^{-A}
 \end{aligned}
\]
by \cite[Proposition~1.14]{Tao_2022}, which holds for $c\cdot\mathbf{Syrac}$ by the transfer. As $m_1\asymp\log^{0.4}x$, the two normalised profiles differ by $O(\log^{-c'}x)$, and \eqref{eq:two-laws-tv} follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:natural}]
For $3\mid c$ we reduce to $c'=c/3$ as in Step~0 of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens}: an odd $n$ lies in $A$ if and only if $(n+c')/2$ lies in $A'=\{m:3m\in A\}$, and $n\mapsto(n+c')/2$ maps the odd integers below $Y$ onto the integers in $[(1+c')/2,(Y+c')/2)$, so that a natural density of $A'$ gives one of the odd part of $A$. Let now $3\nmid c$. For large $N$ put $x=N^{1/\alpha^2}$ and $y=x^\alpha$, so that $\mathbf U_y$ is uniform on the odd integers in $[N^{1/\alpha},N]$. By~\eqref{eq:passage} for $\mathbf U_y$ and for $\mathbf L_y$, by \eqref{eq:two-laws-hit}, by \cite[(1.19)]{Tao_2022} for $T_{3,c}$ and by \eqref{eq:two-laws-tv} with $E=A_1\cap[1,x]$,
\[
 \bigl|\mathbb P(\mathbf U_y\in A_1)-\mathbb P(\mathbf L_y\in A_1)\bigr|\ll(\log x)^{-c'}.
\]
As in the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:natural}, $\mathbb P(\mathbf L_y\in A_1)\to\delta(A)$, the odd integers below $N^{1/\alpha}$ are a proportion $O(N^{1/\alpha-1})$ of those below $N$, and the sets $\{2^km:m\text{ odd}\}$ carry the same relative density. Hence $A$ has the natural density $\delta(A)$.

For a predecessor set $P(a)$, $3\nmid c$, the same argument applies to $A_1=P(a)\cap(2\N+1)$. The first-passage invariance~\eqref{eq:passage} now fails only on the event $\operatorname{Pass}_x=a'$, with $a'$ the odd part of $a$, as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:potential}(a). Its probability under $\mathbf U_y$ is at most its probability under $\mathbf L_y$ plus $O(\log^{-c'}x)$ by~\eqref{eq:two-laws-tv}. Under $\mathbf L_y$ it is $O(\log^{-c'}x)$, because \cite[(5.12)--(5.15)]{Tao_2022} for $T_{3,c}$ give $\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf L_y)\ge x\exp(-O(\log^{0.6}x))>a'$ outside an event of that probability. The numbers $2^ia$ form a set of natural density $0$. Hence $P(a)$ has the natural density $h(a)$.
\end{proof}

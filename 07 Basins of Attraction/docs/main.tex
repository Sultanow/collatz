\begin{abstract}
What determines the global dynamics of a Collatz-type map when its local predecessor structure is indistinguishable from that of another map? We study the maps $x\mapsto(qx+c)/2$ and give three answers. First, every local statistic of the predecessor trees of $3x+1$, including cotree densities, coincides with that of $3x-1$, which has three cycles, so local statistics determine neither the cycles nor the basins. Second, a parity word $B$ of length $l$ with $k$ odd steps yields an integer cycle if and only if $2^l-q^k$ divides $c\,z_q(B)$. One pass over all $2.2\cdot10^{12}$ words of length at most $40$ lists all $1097$ primitive cycles of the maps $3x+c$ with $|c|\le1000$ and confirms the published tables (OEIS A226607) for all cycles with at most $40$ elements. Third, we separate the existence of basin densities from their values. Every basin of every map $3x+c$ with odd $c\ge-1$ has a logarithmic density; in particular, the positive integers whose $3x+1$ orbit reaches $1$ form a set with a logarithmic density. Conditionally on a recent transport estimate of Mazur, this set even has a natural density. For the values, the coset of $n$ modulo the subgroup $\langle2,3\rangle$ of $(\mathbb Z/c\mathbb Z)^\times$ is invariant and fixes many basin densities exactly, and the harmonic mass of the backward tree of a cycle predicts $410$ measured densities to about $5\,\%$, without fitted parameters.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%=====================================================================
Crandall \cite{Crandall_1978} generalized the $3x+1$ sequence by introducing the function $f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:func_qxplus1}
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
qx+1	&	2\nmid x,\\
x/2		&	\text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
for odd $q\geq 3$. More generally, for odd $q\ge1$ and odd $c$ we consider $x\mapsto qx+c$. Since every odd step is followed by at least one halving, we work with the accelerated map
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:T}
T_{q,c}(x)=
\begin{cases}
(qx+c)/2	&	2\nmid x,\\
x/2		&	\text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
which has the same cycles as~\eqref{eq:func_qxplus1} (for $c=1$), up to the omitted even members. We call a ``$qx+c$ cycle'' a sequence of distinct positive integers $(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)$ with $T_{q,c}(x_i)=x_{i+1}$ and $T_{q,c}(x_n)=x_1$.

Leslie Davison observed that \emph{circuits}, i.e.\ cycles consisting of one run of odd steps followed by one run of halvings, correspond one-to-one to the solutions $(h,k,l)$ of the Diophantine equation
\begin{equation}
(2^{k+l}-3^k)h=2^l-1,
\label{eq:diophantine_davison}
\end{equation}
see \cite[p.~67]{Lagarias_2010}. Steiner \cite{Steiner_1977} proved that $(h,k,l)=(1,1,1)$ is the only solution, so the trivial cycle is the only circuit of $3x+1$. Simons and de Weger \cite{Simons_deWeger_2005} and Hercher \cite{Hercher_2023} extended this to $m$-cycles (cycles with $m$ runs of odd steps) for $m\le 68$ and $m\le 91$, respectively. The general cycle problem remains open; convergence has been verified for all $n<2^{71}$ \cite{Barina_2025}.

For $q>3$ cycles are rare. Table~\ref{table:known_cycles} lists the non-trivial cycles of~\eqref{eq:func_qxplus1} for odd $q\le1000$.

Let $(a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_n)$ be the odd members of a cycle of~\eqref{eq:func_qxplus1} in cyclic order. Then the product
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:condition_cycle_existance}
2^\alpha=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(q+\frac{1}{a_i}\right)
\end{equation}
is a power of two, see for example \cite[p.~327]{Lagarias_2010}. Here $\alpha$ is the number of divisions by $2$ in one period, and $n$ is the number of odd members.

\subsection*{Related work}
Cycles of the maps $3x+d$ have been tabulated before. Lagarias \cite{Lagarias_1990} introduced primitive cycles, whose members are coprime to $d$, and conjectured that every map $3x+d$ has at least one and only finitely many of them. Belaga and Mignotte \cite{Belaga_Mignotte_2000,Belaga_Mignotte_2006} listed the primitive cycles for all $d<20000$, completely as they argue, and Morley \cite{OEIS_A226607} confirmed these lists by following every trajectory with starting value below $10^8$. Belaga and Mignotte also embedded the $3x+1$ conjecture in the $3x+d$ context \cite{Belaga_Mignotte_1998} and described the cycles of all maps $3x+d$ at once, through a duality between the shift $d$ and the members of a cycle \cite{Belaga_Mignotte_DMTCS_2006}. Cox \cite{Cox_2012} modelled the number of cycles per $d$ by a Poisson distribution. These lists are conjectural: a cycle whose basin contains no small starting value would be missed. The census of Section~\ref{sec:zoo} is complete by construction for bounded word length, and it confirms the published lists in that range.

Densities enter through predecessor sets. Wirsching \cite{Wirsching_1998} studied the density of the set of integers that iterate to a fixed integer under $3x+1$ with $3$-adic methods. Tao \cite{Tao_2022} proved that almost all orbits attain almost bounded values, in the sense of logarithmic density, and Gon\c{c}alves, Greenfeld and Madrid \cite{GGM_2025} extended this to a class of maps that contains $3x+c$ for odd $c\ge-1$. They also observed numerically that the three cycles of $3x-1$ attract about a third of all integers each, and asked whether such densities exist \cite[Remark~1.7]{GGM_2025}; their question concerns natural densities. Mazur \cite{Mazur_2026} recently upgraded Tao's theorem to natural density, with an explicit logarithmic time bound, by comparing the first-passage laws of natural and logarithmic counting.

On the local side, Kleinnijenhuis, Kleinnijenhuis and Aydogan \cite{Kleinnijenhuis_2026} develop a graph-theoretic approach to the $an+b$ conjectures. They represent these maps by Cayley trees with Leftward and Upward edges and three-colour pictures, with a detailed congruence-class bookkeeping that includes cotree densities. Section~\ref{sec:local} adopts this tree perspective.

\subsection*{Two views of the same maps}
These maps admit two descriptions. The \emph{local} view starts from the trivial cycle and grows the tree of all predecessors, organised by congruence classes. It describes how numbers branch, and it is the same for every map in the family: by $x\mapsto -x$, the $3x-1$ map on the positive integers is the $3x+1$ map on the negative integers, which has the three cycles through $-1$, $-5$ and $-17$. Both maps produce the same branching statistics (Proposition~\ref{prop:local}). In the language of Plato's cave, they cast identical shadows. Density arguments built on this view reach ``almost all'' statements \cite{Terras_1976,Tao_2022}, but they cannot decide individual orbits.

The \emph{global} view encodes a cycle by its parity word and asks whether the corresponding rational number is a positive integer. This is decided by the arithmetic of $2^l-q^k$, and it is exactly what separates $3x+1$ from $3x-1$.

The guiding question of this paper is therefore: \emph{what determines the global dynamics of a Collatz-type map when its local predecessor structure is indistinguishable from that of another map?} Sections~\ref{sec:local}--\ref{sec:basins} give three complementary answers. The local structure of $3x+1$ and $3x-1$ is statistically identical. Cycles are characterised by an exact global divisibility condition. The existence of basin densities is common to all maps of the family, but their values differ strongly and are so far unexplained.

\subsection*{Main results}
For $A\subseteq\N$ let $\delta(A)=\lim_{N\to\infty}(\log N)^{-1}\sum_{n<N,\,n\in A}1/n$ be its logarithmic density, if the limit exists. Our main theoretical result separates the existence of basin densities from their values. Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} states that every set of positive integers that is completely invariant under $T_{3,c}$, with odd $c\ge-1$, has a logarithmic density; in particular, every basin of attraction has one. Existence is therefore common to all maps of the family, whereas the values separate them: numerically, the three basins of $3x-1$ have nearly equal densities, while two cycles of the same type in $3x+5$ attract about $50\,\%$ and $9\,\%$ of all integers. For the Collatz map itself, Corollary~\ref{cor:collatz} shows that the positive integers whose orbit reaches $1$ form a set with a logarithmic density. This does not decide the Collatz conjecture, which asserts that the set is $\N$, but it shows unconditionally that the logarithmic proportion of these integers converges. Conditionally on a recent natural-counting transport estimate of Mazur \cite{Mazur_2026}, the density is even a natural density (Corollary~\ref{cor:natural}).

The proof is short. A basin is not changed by the first passage of an orbit below a threshold, so the stabilisation of first passages of Gon\c{c}alves, Greenfeld and Madrid \cite{GGM_2025}, after Tao \cite{Tao_2022}, makes the basin shares of consecutive logarithmic windows converge; two windows of incommensurable ratio then exclude oscillations in $\log\log N$. To our knowledge, these existence statements have not been made before. How to compute the values from the global dynamics remains the central open problem (Problem~\ref{prob:basins}). Section~\ref{sec:basins} gives two partial answers. An elementary multiplicative invariant fixes the densities of many basins exactly (Proposition~\ref{prop:cosets}); for a prime $p\equiv\pm1\pmod{24}$, for example, the Legendre symbol $(n/p)$ is constant along the orbits of $3x+p$. And the harmonic mass of the backward tree of a cycle, a heuristic quantity computed from the cycle alone, predicts the measured densities of $410$ cycles to about $5\,\%$ (Figure~\ref{fig:predictor}).

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
	\item Section~\ref{sec:local} makes the limits of local methods precise. All densities of locally defined sets coincide for $3x+1$ and $3x-1$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:local}), so local branching statistics, including the cotree densities of Cayley trees, determine neither the number of cycles nor basin membership (Corollary~\ref{cor:local}). This limits proof strategies that rely only on such statistics, not proofs that combine predecessor trees with global information.
	\item Section~\ref{sec:words} recalls the classical correspondence between parity words and cycles (Lemma~\ref{lem:words}, due to B\"ohm--Sontacchi and Lagarias) in the notation of rotating binaries, and records the families of cycles obtained from binary rotations, the mirror symmetry and the product identity~\eqref{eq:diophantine_equation}.
	\item Section~\ref{sec:zoo} takes the reverse perspective of Lagarias' rational cycles and of the shift--member duality of Belaga and Mignotte: instead of asking which cycles a given map has, it asks for which maps a given parity word yields a cycle. What is new is the exhaustive evaluation of this correspondence. One pass over all $2.2\cdot10^{12}$ words of length at most $40$ lists all $1097$ primitive cycles of all maps $3x+c$ with $|c|\le1000$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:rational}). For $0<c<1000$ it proves the published tables complete for all cycles with at most $40$ elements. An equidistribution heuristic predicts the counts, with less scatter than a Poisson model (Figure~\ref{fig:cycles}, Problem~\ref{prob:heuristic}).
	\item Section~\ref{sec:basins} proves that every basin has a logarithmic density (Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens}); in particular, the integers whose $3x+1$ orbit reaches $1$ form a set with a logarithmic density (Corollary~\ref{cor:collatz}). The proof combines the invariance of basins under first passage with the stabilisation of first passages of Gon\c{c}alves, Greenfeld and Madrid, and excludes oscillations in $\log\log y$ by comparing two windows of incommensurable ratio. Conditionally on the natural-counting transport estimate of Mazur, this becomes a natural density for $3x+1$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:natural}). The section measures the basin densities of $18$ maps up to $10^{10}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:basins}) and, by sampling first passages, those of three maps at scales up to $10^{220}$ (Table~\ref{table:passage}). An elementary divisibility filtration (Proposition~\ref{prop:filtration}) gives the densities of the gcd classes and explains the rational entries of Table~\ref{table:basins}. A multiplicative filtration by the cosets of the subgroup generated by $2$ and $3$ in $(\mathbb Z/c\mathbb Z)^\times$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:cosets}) fixes the densities of further basins exactly; a survey of all $133$ maps $3x+c$ with $c\le400$ confirms it in every case (Figure~\ref{fig:landscape}). The harmonic mass of the backward tree of a cycle predicts the remaining values to about $5\,\%$, with an error that decreases steadily with the depth (Table~\ref{table:convergence}, Figure~\ref{fig:predictor}). We formulate the existence of natural densities and a proof of the predictor as open problems (Problem~\ref{prob:basins}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Local structure: one forest, several trees}
\label{sec:local}
%=====================================================================
The local view grows the tree of all predecessors of a cycle. For the Collatz map this tree is usually restricted to the \emph{branching numbers}, the even numbers with an odd predecessor, and drawn as a Cayley tree with \emph{Leftward} edges through an odd predecessor and \emph{Upward} edges by doubling \cite{Sultanow_graph_2020}. The congruence-class bookkeeping of these trees for general $an+b$ maps, including their \emph{cotree densities}, is developed in detail by Kleinnijenhuis et al.\ \cite{Kleinnijenhuis_2026}. In this section we record two facts that hold for every map of the family alike.

Let $c$ be coprime to $6$. A \emph{branching number} is an even $n\in\N$ such that $(n-c)/3$ is an odd integer not divisible by $3$; let $S_c$ be the set of branching numbers. For $m\in S_c$ define the \emph{Leftward child} $L(m)=2^p\,(m-c)/3$ and the \emph{Upward child} $U(m)=2^r m$, where $p,r\ge1$ are minimal with $L(m),U(m)\in S_c$. Both exist because $2$ is a primitive root modulo $9$. The following tree property of the predecessor graph is standard; we include its short proof in the present notation.

\begin{proposition}[Unique parent]
\label{prop:unique_parent}
The maps $L,U:S_c\to S_c$ are injective, and $S_c$ is the disjoint union of $L(S_c)$ and $U(S_c)$. In other words, every branching number has exactly one parent, either as a Leftward or as an Upward child.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $n\in S_c$ and consider $n/2,n/4,\ldots$ up to the first term that is odd or lies in $S_c$. If this term is $n/2^r\in S_c$, then $m=n/2^r$ satisfies $U(m)=n$: the exponent $r$ is minimal, because an earlier term in $S_c$ would contradict the choice of $r$. If the first such term is an odd number $o=n/2^p$, then $3\nmid o$, since $n\equiv c\pmod 3$ and $3\nmid c$. Hence $m=3o+c$ is even, $(m-c)/3=o$ is odd and not divisible by $3$, so $m\in S_c$, and $L(m)=n$ by the same minimality argument. The two cases exclude each other, and in each case the parent is determined by $n$. This proves surjectivity onto the disjoint union and injectivity.
\end{proof}

For $c=\pm1$, the halving sequence in this proof stops after at most four steps, so whether $n$ is a Leftward or an Upward child depends only on $n\bmod 2^4\cdot18=288$. For $3x+1$, Kleinnijenhuis et al.\ count $27$ Leftward and $5$ Upward classes modulo $288$ \cite[(3.73), (3.79)]{Kleinnijenhuis_2026}, which gives the cotree density
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:forest_density}
d\bigl(L(S_c)\bigr)+d\bigl(U(S_c)\bigr)=\frac{27}{288}+\frac{5}{288}=\frac{2}{18}=d(S_c).
\end{equation}
The same counts hold for $3x-1$, whose Upward classes are the negatives of those of $3x+1$ modulo $288$ (Table~\ref{table:mirror}). The identity~\eqref{eq:forest_density} gives the density of the whole forest $S_c$, not of the tree of the trivial cycle: for $3x-1$ that tree contains only about a third of all integers (Section~\ref{sec:basins}).

\subsection*{Local indistinguishability}
Let $\iota(n)=-n$. Then $T_{3,-1}=\iota\circ T_{3,1}\circ\iota$ on $\Z$. We call a set $A\subseteq\N$ \emph{locally defined} for a map $T$ if, for some fixed $m$ and modulus $M$, membership of $n$ in $A$ depends only on the residues of $n,T(n),\ldots,T^m(n)$ modulo $M$. The branching numbers, the Leftward and Upward children and the cotree classes are locally defined, and so is, for every word $B$ of length $m$, the set of $n$ whose first $m$ parities form $B$.

\begin{proposition}[Local indistinguishability]
\label{prop:local}
Let $A\subseteq\N$ be locally defined for $T_{3,1}$ by residue conditions $R$, and let $A^-\subseteq\N$ be the set defined for $T_{3,-1}$ by the negated conditions $-R$. Then $A$ and $A^-$ are periodic sets with the same natural density.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $T=T_{3,\pm1}$, the residue of $T(n)$ modulo $M$ depends only on $n$ modulo $2M$, hence the residue of $T^i(n)$ modulo $M$ depends only on $n$ modulo $2^iM$. Thus $A=\{n\in\N: n\bmod 2^mM\in Q\}$ for a set $Q$ of residues, and the same condition defines a periodic set $\tilde A\subseteq\Z$ for $T_{3,1}$ on all of $\Z$. Since $T_{3,-1}(n)=-T_{3,1}(-n)$, we have $n\in A^-$ if and only if $-n\in\tilde A$, that is, $A^-=\{n\in\N:-n\bmod 2^mM\in Q\}$. Both sets have density $|Q|/(2^mM)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:local}
A statement about $T_{3,1}$ that is formulated only in terms of the densities of locally defined sets holds if and only if the corresponding statement holds for $T_{3,-1}$. In particular, local branching statistics determine neither the number of cycles nor basin membership: $3x+1$ has one known cycle and $3x-1$ has three (Section~\ref{sec:zoo}), and Table~\ref{table:classes} shows that membership in a basin is not a function of any residue class.
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}[Scope]
Corollary~\ref{cor:local} limits proof strategies that use only local branching statistics, such as cotree densities. It does not exclude proofs that combine the predecessor tree with global information that differs between $3x+1$ and $3x-1$: the size of the numbers involved, as in the descent $(x+1)/2<x$ for the map $x+1$, or the arithmetic of $2^l-3^k$ (Section~\ref{sec:words}).
\end{remark}

\subsection*{Residue classes do not decide membership}
Which tree of the forest a branching number belongs to is not a property of its residue class, for none of the moduli $18$, $288$ and $4608$ used in this kind of bookkeeping. Table~\ref{table:classes} shows this for $3x-1$, where all trees are known: members of the same class lie in different trees, in both directions. A check of the representatives $\ell=0$ can therefore refute a conjecture (the number $14$ refutes it for $3x-1$), but it cannot confirm one. For $3x+1$, the representatives $4$ and $16$ lie in the tree of the root; whether all members of $18\ell+4$ and $18\ell+16$ do is the Collatz conjecture itself. Section~\ref{sec:basins} shows more: for $3x-1$, $3x+5$ and $3x+13$, residue classes modulo powers of $2$ and $3$ do not even shift the basin shares measurably.

\section{Parity words and cycles}
\label{sec:words}
%=====================================================================
Fix odd $q\ge1$ and odd $c$, and write $T=T_{q,c}$. A \emph{word} $B=b_0b_1\cdots b_{l-1}\in\{0,1\}^l$ prescribes the parities of $l$ consecutive iterates. Its \emph{type} is $(l,k)$ with $k=b_0+\cdots+b_{l-1}$, the number of odd steps; we write
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:D}
D=D(l,k)=2^l-q^k .
\end{equation}
Let $0\le p_1<p_2<\cdots<p_k\le l-1$ be the positions of the ones in $B$; the position $0$ refers to the first step. The rotating-binary function of Cox et al.\ \cite{Cox_2021}, adapted from Halbeisen and Hungerbühler \cite{Halbeisen_Hungerbuehler_1997}, is
\begin{equation}
z_q(B) = \sum_{j=1}^{k}q^{\,k-j}\,2^{p_j}.
\label{eq:z}
\end{equation}
Let $\sigma B=b_1\cdots b_{l-1}b_0$ denote the left rotation of $B$, and let $\Zt$ be the ring of rationals with odd denominator. The following correspondence is classical \cite{Boehm_Sontacchi_1978,Lagarias_1990}; we state it in the notation of rotating binaries and sketch the proof.

\begin{lemma}[Cycles from words; B\"ohm--Sontacchi, Lagarias]
\label{lem:words}
Let $B$ be a word of type $(l,k)$ with $D\neq0$, and put
\[
x_B=\frac{c\,z_q(B)}{D}\in\Zt .
\]
Then $x_B$ is a periodic point of $T$ (extended to $\Zt$) whose parity sequence is $BBB\cdots$, and it is the only such point in $\Zt$. The orbit of $x_B$ is $x_B, x_{\sigma B}, x_{\sigma^2B},\ldots$. Consequently:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item $T$ has an integer cycle with parity word $B$ if and only if $D\mid c\,z_q(B)$;
	\item for $k\ge1$ this cycle consists of positive integers if and only if $c\,D>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Sketch of proof]
Let $T_0(x)=x/2$, $T_1(x)=(qx+c)/2$ and $T_B=T_{b_{l-1}}\circ\cdots\circ T_{b_0}$. By induction on $l$, $T_B(x)=(q^kx+c\,z_q(B))/2^l$, whose unique fixed point is $x_B$; it lies in $\Zt$ because $D$ is odd. From $T_{\sigma B}\circ T_{b_0}=T_{b_0}\circ T_B$ we get $T_{b_0}(x_B)=x_{\sigma B}$, and $2x_{\sigma B}=q^{b_0}x_B+b_0c$ shows that $x_B$ has parity $b_0$. Hence the orbit of $x_B$ follows $B$ periodically. If $y\in\Zt$ also has parity sequence $BBB\cdots$, then $T^{jl}(y)-T^{jl}(x_B)=(q^k/2^l)^j(y-x_B)$ lies in $\Zt$ for all $j$, so $y=x_B$. Statements (a) and (b) follow because $z_q(B)>0$ for $k\ge1$.
\end{proof}

Every positive integer cycle of $T$ arises in this way from the word of its parities, and its members are obtained by rotating the word. This is the precise sense in which cycles are \emph{rotating binaries}. The reverse direction, asking for which maps a given word yields a cycle, is also classical: it is Lagarias' theory of rational cycles \cite{Lagarias_1990} and the shift--member duality of Belaga and Mignotte \cite[Theorem~4]{Belaga_Mignotte_DMTCS_2006}.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:971}
Take $q=5$ and the word $B=111110000000$ of type $(12,5)$. Then $D=2^{12}-5^5=971$. With $c=971$ we get $x_B=z_5(B)=5^4+5^3\cdot2+5^2\cdot2^2+5\cdot2^3+2^4=1031$, and the twelve rotations of $B$ produce the cycle
\[
(1031,3063,8143,20843,52593,131968,65984,32992,16496,8248,4124,2062)
\]
of $5x+971$. Its odd members are $1031,3063,8143,20843,52593$. The binary representation of $B$ reflects the course of the cycle: the ones mark the odd members.
\end{example}

\paragraph{Words with $D=c$.} If $c=2^l-q^k>0$, then $D=c$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:words} gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:D_equals_c}
x_B=z_q(B)\qquad\text{for every word $B$ of type $(l,k)$.}
\end{equation}
Every such word therefore yields a positive integer cycle of $T_{q,c}$, with members $z_q(\sigma^iB)$, and each necklace (rotation class) of type $(l,k)$ gives one cycle. This explains the cycles of Table~\ref{table:non_q1_cycles}, where $7=2^5-5^2$, $103=2^7-5^2$, $3=2^7-5^3$ and $131=2^8-5^3$, and the cycle families of $3x+5$ ($5=2^5-3^3$) and $3x+13$ ($13=2^8-3^5$) in Section~\ref{sec:zoo}.

\paragraph{Mirror and $D=1$.} The conjugation $x\mapsto-x$ gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mirror}
T_{q,-c}(x)=-T_{q,c}(-x),
\end{equation}
so the cycles of $3x-1$ are the negatives of the cycles of $3x+1$ on the negative integers. By Lemma~\ref{lem:words}(b) they come from words with $D<0$: the three cycles of $3x-1$ through $1$, $5$ and $17$ have the words $1$, $110$ and $11110111000$, with $D=-1$, $-1$ and $-139$ (since $2^3<3^2$ and $2^{11}<3^7$). If $q=2^m-1$, the word $10^{m-1}$ has $D=2^m-q=1$ and $z_q=1$, so $x_B=c$; for $c=1$ the number $1$ lies on a cycle. This is the power-of-two root $c=q+1=2^m$ of the family $a=2^m-1$ studied in \cite{Kleinnijenhuis_2026}.

\begin{remark}[The case $q=1$]
For $q=1$ we have $z_1(B)=\sum_j 2^{p_j}$, the integer whose binary digits, least significant first, are $b_0,\ldots,b_{l-1}$. For $c=2^l-1$, the map $T_{1,c}$ acts on $\{0,\ldots,2^l-1\}$ as the cyclic right rotation of $l$-bit numbers. For example, the cycles of $x+7$ are $\{1,4,2\}$, $\{3,5,6\}$ and $\{7\}$, the rotation classes of $001$, $011$ and $111$. For $c=1$, by contrast, $0<z_1(B)<2^l-1$ unless $B=1^l$, so $T_{1,1}$ has only the cycle $\{1\}$. This also follows from the descent $(x+1)/2<x$ for odd $x>1$, a global, Archimedean argument. This is the trivial base case $1n+1$ of the family $an+b$.
\end{remark}

\subsection*{The product identity}
Let $a_1,\ldots,a_k$ be the odd members of a positive cycle of~\eqref{eq:func_qxplus1} of type $(l,k)$, in cyclic order, and put $A_i=qa_i$. Dividing~\eqref{eq:condition_cycle_existance} by $q^k$ gives
\[
\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Bigl(1+\frac1{A_i}\Bigr)=\frac{2^l}{q^k},
\]
and for odd $q>1$ the right-hand side determines $(l,k)$. Hence the members of two cycles satisfy the product equality
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:diophantine_equation}
(A_1+1)\cdots(A_m+1)\cdot B_1\cdots B_n=A_1\cdots A_m\cdot(B_1+1)\cdots(B_n+1)
\end{equation}
if and only if the cycles have the same type. For example, the two non-trivial cycles of $5x+1$ both have type $(7,3)$, so~\eqref{eq:diophantine_equation} holds for $A=5\cdot(13,33,83)$ and $B=5\cdot(27,17,43)$; both products equal $2^7/5^3$. Likewise, both cycles of $181x+1$ have type $(15,2)$ (Table~\ref{table:zoo}).

\subsection*{Near-coincidences}
In every row of Table~\ref{table:zoo} with a non-trivial cycle, $|D|$ is small compared with $2^l$. For example, $2^{15}-181^2=32768-32761=7$, $2^7-5^3=128-125=3$ and $|D|/2^l=0.038$ for the type $(27,17)$ of $3x+5$. Equivalently, $l/k$ is a good rational approximation of $\log_2 q$:
\[
\begin{array}{rcll}
15/2 &=& 7.5 & \approx \log_2 181 = 7.49985,\\
7/3 &=& 2.333 & \approx \log_2 5 = 2.32193,\\
27/17,\ 8/5,\ 11/7 &=& 1.588,\ 1.600,\ 1.571 & \approx \log_2 3 = 1.58496 .
\end{array}
\]
For $3x-1$ the approximations come from below ($2^l<3^k$), in accordance with~\eqref{eq:mirror}. The heuristic count $E$ explains the pattern. A small $|D|$ makes the divisibility $D\mid c\,z_q(B)$ likely, while a large $|D|$ needs an astronomical number of words.

\subsection*{The near miss of \texorpdfstring{$3x+1$}{3x+1}}
The type $(8,5)$ illustrates where the $3x+1$ problem is decided.

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:near_miss}
The $3x+1$ map has no cycle of type $(8,5)$, and the seven cycles of $3x+13$ of this type are exactly the seven candidates.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:words}, a $3x+1$ cycle of type $(8,5)$ requires $13=2^8-3^5$ to divide $z_3(B)$ for a word $B$ of that type. For $c=13$ we have $D=c$, so by~\eqref{eq:D_equals_c} the values $z_3(B)$ are exactly the members of the seven cycles of $3x+13$ of type $(8,5)$. Their minima $211,227,251,259,283,287,319$ are congruent to $3,6,4,12,10,1,7$ modulo $13$. Since $T_{3,13}$ maps non-multiples of $13$ to non-multiples of $13$, no member of these cycles is divisible by $13$.
\end{proof}

The heuristic expectation for this type is $E=7/13\approx0.54$; the actual count is $0$. Belaga and Mignotte call $2^l-3^k$ a Collatz number and the values $z_3(B)$ of the words of type $(l,k)$ that begin with an odd step its corona. In their language, Corollary~\ref{cor:near_miss} states that the Collatz number $2^8-3^5=13$ divides no member of its corona; they showed that the absence of non-trivial positive $3x+1$ cycles is equivalent to this property for every non-trivial type \cite[Theorem~5]{Belaga_Mignotte_DMTCS_2006}. For longer types, the methods of Steiner, Simons--de Weger and Hercher \cite{Steiner_1977,Simons_deWeger_2005,Hercher_2023} combine lower bounds for $|l\log2-k\log3|$ from Baker's theory with the verification limit. These bounds show that $D$ is too large for the divisibility to occur in cycles with few runs. Whatever proves the absence of cycles must see this arithmetic. The local structure of Section~\ref{sec:local} does not see it, because it is identical for $3x+1$ and $3x+13$ up to scaling.

\subsection*{All maps at once: rational cycles}
We now take the reverse perspective: instead of asking which cycles a given map has, we ask for which maps a given parity word yields a cycle. This is the point of view of Lagarias' rational cycles \cite{Lagarias_1990} and of the shift--member duality of Belaga and Mignotte \cite[Section~7]{Belaga_Mignotte_DMTCS_2006}. Lemma~\ref{lem:words} answers the question for every word. Let $d_0$ be the denominator of $z_3(B)/D$ in lowest terms. Then $x_B=c\,z_3(B)/D$ is a positive integer if and only if $c=\operatorname{sign}(D)\,m\,d_0$ with $m$ odd, and the cycle is $m$ times the \emph{primitive} cycle of $3x+c_0$, $c_0=\operatorname{sign}(D)\,d_0$. These are Lagarias' rational cycles of $3x+1$ with denominator $d_0$ \cite{Lagarias_1990}. A single pass over all words therefore lists the primitive cycles of all maps with $|c_0|\le C$. The denominator comes from the continued-fraction expansion of $z_3(B)/|D|$, which can be stopped as soon as a convergent has a denominator larger than $C$.

\begin{proposition}[Rational cycles]
\label{prop:rational}
For word length $l\le40$ there are exactly $1097$ primitive cycles of the maps $3x+c_0$ with $|c_0|\le1000$: $763$ with $c_0>0$ and $334$ with $c_0<0$, for $199$ distinct values of $|c_0|$. Every cycle of a map $3x+c$ with $|c|\le1000$ and word length at most $40$ is an odd multiple of one of them.
\end{proposition}

The computation checked all $2\,199\,023\,255\,510$ words in $512$ shards of about six minutes each on the HPC cluster of the EDIH Hamburg, with a parity check of every reported cycle. The program was validated against an independent implementation in exact rational arithmetic (for $l\le16$ and $|c_0|\le50$) and against the single-map census for $3x\pm1$, $3x+5$ and $3x+13$.

\paragraph{Comparison with the published tables.} For each of the $333$ values $0<k<1000$ with $\gcd(k,6)=1$, the primitive cycles of $3x+k$ listed by Belaga, Mignotte and Morley \cite{Belaga_Mignotte_2006,OEIS_A226607} that have at most $40$ elements coincide exactly with those of Proposition~\ref{prop:rational}; there are $763$ of them. The other $462$ listed cycles have between $41$ and $426$ elements, and for $153$ of these maps every known primitive cycle is longer than $40$. Proposition~\ref{prop:rational} therefore turns the conjecturally complete tables into a theorem for all cycles with at most $40$ elements and $k<1000$.

\paragraph{Heuristic.} If $z_3(B)$ were equidistributed modulo $|D|$, the reduced denominator of $z_3(B)/|D|$ would equal a divisor $e$ of $|D|$ with probability $\varphi(e)/|D|$. A primitive cycle corresponds to one aperiodic necklace of words, so the expected number of primitive cycles of type $(l,k)$ with $|c_0|\le C$ is
\[
E_C(l,k)=L(l,k)\,\frac{S_C(D)}{|D|},\qquad S_C(D)=\sum_{e\,\mid\,|D|,\ e\le C}\varphi(e),
\]
where $L(l,k)$ is the number of aperiodic binary necklaces of type $(l,k)$. If all divisors of $|D|$ are at most $C$, then $S_C(D)=|D|$, every necklace yields a cycle, and $E_C$ is exact; for example, $D(10,5)=781=11\cdot71$ gives all $25$ necklaces. Summed over $l\le40$, the heuristic predicts $1148.7$ primitive cycles against $1097$ observed, and it reproduces the counts per word length including the peaks (Figure~\ref{fig:cycles}). The agreement has a deterministic and a statistical part. For the $44$ types with $|D|\le1000$, all divisors of $|D|$ are admissible, every necklace yields a cycle, and the $227$ cycles of these types are predicted exactly. For the other $116$ types with $E_{1000}\ge1$, the heuristic predicts $879.8$ cycles and we observe $839$; the remaining types with $E_{1000}<1$ contribute $41.9$ expected and $31$ observed cycles. For the $116$ types, $\sum(O-E)^2/E=71.4$, a dispersion index of $0.62$, nearly constant across the ranges $1\le E<3$, $3\le E<10$ and $E\ge10$. The counts thus scatter less than a Poisson model allows. This suggests that the residues $z_3(B)\bmod|D|$ of the words of a type are distributed more evenly than independent random residues would be.

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:heuristic}
Why does the equidistribution heuristic predict the number of primitive cycles so well, and why are the counts underdispersed? Can the distribution of $z_3(B)$ modulo $|D|$ over the words of a type be controlled?
\end{problem}

\paragraph{Longer words.} The census is complete for $l\le40$ only. A search over the starting values $n<10^5$ of the maps of Section~\ref{sec:basins} finds three further cycles: the cycle of $3x+23$ through $41$ ($l=43$) and two cycles of $3x+29$ through $3811$ and $7055$ ($l=65$, $k=41$). The last two sit exactly at the convergent $65/41$ of $\log_2 3$, the one after $19/12$.

\section{Measuring forests: basin densities}
\label{sec:basins}
%=====================================================================
For a cycle $\mathcal C$ of $T_{q,c}$, its \emph{basin} is $\beta(\mathcal C)=\{n\in\N: T^j(n)\in\mathcal C \text{ for some } j\}$, and we write $d_N(\mathcal C)=|\beta(\mathcal C)\cap[1,N]|/N$. The basins are the trees of the forest of Section~\ref{sec:local}; the local structure fixes only the total density of all trees. We report empirical shares $d_N$. Except for the basins covered by Propositions~\ref{prop:filtration} and~\ref{prop:cosets}, it is not known whether the natural density $\lim_{N\to\infty}d_N$ exists; Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} below shows that the logarithmic density always does.

Two entries of Table~\ref{table:basins} are rational, $0.20000=1/5$ and $0.07692\approx1/13$. They are explained by the following elementary gcd invariance, which is implicit in Lagarias' primitive cycles \cite{Lagarias_1990} and explicit in \cite[(19)]{Belaga_Mignotte_DMTCS_2006}.

\begin{proposition}[Divisibility filtration]
\label{prop:filtration}
Let $\gcd(c,6)=1$ and, for a divisor $e$ of $|c|$, let $B_e=\{n\ge1:\gcd(n,c)=e\}$. Then $T_{3,c}(B_e)\subseteq B_e$, the map $n\mapsto n/e$ conjugates $T_{3,c}$ on $B_e$ to $T_{3,c/e}$ on the integers coprime to $c/e$, and $B_e$ has natural density $\varphi(|c|/e)/|c|$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Every prime divisor $p$ of $c$ satisfies $p\ge5$. Since $p\mid(3n+c)/2$ if and only if $p\mid n$, and $p\mid n/2$ if and only if $p\mid n$, the map preserves $\gcd(n,c)$. For odd $e$, the numbers $em$ and $m$ have the same parity, and $T_{3,c}(em)=e\,T_{3,c/e}(m)$. Finally, $n=em$ lies in $B_e$ if and only if $\gcd(m,c/e)=1$, a condition of density $\varphi(|c|/e)/(|c|/e)$ for $m$, hence of density $\varphi(|c|/e)/|c|$ for $n$.
\end{proof}

Consequently, for every cycle $\mathcal C$ of $3x+c/e$ coprime to $c/e$, the basin of $e\,\mathcal C$ in $3x+c$ has exactly $1/e$ times the density of the basin of $\mathcal C$ in $3x+c/e$, whenever the latter exists. The divisibility filtration has a multiplicative refinement. For $c$ coprime to $6$, let $G=(\mathbb Z/c\mathbb Z)^\times$ and let $H$ be the subgroup of $G$ generated by $2$ and $3$.

\begin{proposition}[Multiplicative filtration]
\label{prop:cosets}
Let $c\ge1$ with $\gcd(c,6)=1$. For every coset $gH$, the set $B_{1,gH}=\{n\in\N:\gcd(n,c)=1,\ n\bmod c\in gH\}$ is completely invariant under $T_{3,c}$, and it has natural density $\varphi(c)/(c\,[G:H])$. In particular, if $p\equiv\pm1\pmod{24}$ is prime, the Legendre symbol $(n/p)$ is constant along every orbit of $T_{3,p}$ on the integers prime to $p$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $n$ prime to $c$, $T_{3,c}(n)$ is congruent to $2^{-1}n$ or to $3\cdot2^{-1}n$ modulo $c$, and both factors lie in $H$. Conversely, every preimage of an integer prime to $c$ is prime to $c$. Hence $B_{1,gH}$ is completely invariant. It is the union of $|H|$ residue classes modulo $c$, so its density is $|H|/c=\varphi(c)/(c\,[G:H])$. For a prime $p\equiv\pm1\pmod{24}$, both $2$ and $3$ are quadratic residues modulo $p$, so $H$ consists of quadratic residues.
\end{proof}

The proposition applies within every gcd class $B_e$ as well, with $c/e$ in place of $c$. If a cycle is the only cycle in its coset, its basin is the whole coset, up to orbits that never reach a cycle, and has the rational density $\varphi(c)/(c\,[G:H])$. The invariance is elementary and closely related to the Markov chains on residue classes of generalized Collatz maps studied by Matthews \cite{Matthews_2010}; we have not found it stated for the maps $3x+c$. It is confirmed numerically below.

\begin{proposition}[Verification]
\label{prop:verification}
For $3x-1$ and the $17$ maps $3x+c$ with $0<c<50$ and $\gcd(c,6)=1$, every orbit of $1\le n\le10^{10}$ reaches one of the cycles of Proposition~\ref{prop:rational} or one of the three cycles with longer words found above.
\end{proposition}

The right panel follows the three basins of $3x-1$ through the dyadic windows $[2^j,2^{j+1})$, and in the window $[2^{32},2^{33})$ the densities of all basins differ from those up to $10^{10}$ by at most $2.4\cdot10^{-4}$. The type of a cycle, its length and number of odd steps, therefore does not determine its attractiveness.

\subsection*{Existence of the densities}
For $A\subseteq\N$, the \emph{logarithmic density} is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:logdens}
\delta(A)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{\log N}\sum_{\substack{n<N\\ n\in A}}\frac1n,
\end{equation}
if the limit exists. If the natural density of $A$ exists, then $\delta(A)$ exists and has the same value; the converse fails in general. Tao proved his theorem on almost bounded orbits in logarithmic density \cite{Tao_2022}, and Gon\c{c}alves, Greenfeld and Madrid extended his method to a class of maps that contains $T_{3,c}$ \cite{GGM_2025}. They observed numerically that the predecessor set of an integer $a$ appears to have a natural density $\kappa(a)$, and that each of the three cycles of $3x-1$ attracts about a third of all integers; they also proposed a formulation with logarithmically distributed starting values \cite[Remark~1.7]{GGM_2025}. In this logarithmic form, existence follows from their stabilisation of first passages, because a basin is not changed by first passage. For natural densities see Corollary~\ref{cor:natural} and Remark~\ref{rem:mazur}.

We call $A\subseteq\N$ \emph{completely invariant} under $T$ if $n\in A\iff T(n)\in A$. Every basin is completely invariant, and so is the set of starting values with unbounded orbits.

\begin{theorem}[Logarithmic basin densities]
\label{thm:logdens}
Let $c\ge-1$ be odd, and let $A\subseteq\N$ be completely invariant under $T_{3,c}$. Then $\delta(A)$ exists. In particular, the basin of every cycle $\mathcal C$ of $T_{3,c}$ has a logarithmic density $\delta(\mathcal C)$, and if $\lim_{N\to\infty}d_N(\mathcal C)$ exists, it equals $\delta(\mathcal C)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[The basin of $1$ for $3x+1$]
\label{cor:collatz}
The positive integers whose $3x+1$ orbit reaches $1$ form a set with a logarithmic density.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $c=1$ this set is the basin of the cycle $(1,2)$ of $T_{3,1}$, which is completely invariant.
\end{proof}

The Collatz conjecture asserts that this set is $\N$, so that its density is $1$. Corollary~\ref{cor:collatz} does not decide this, but it shows unconditionally that the logarithmic proportion of these integers up to $N$ converges. We have not found this consequence stated in the literature. Corollary~\ref{cor:natural} below upgrades it to natural density, conditionally on Mazur's transport estimate. For maps with several basins, such as $3x-1$ and $3x+5$, Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} gives the existence of the densities of all basins, whose values no conjecture predicts.

For the proof we use the Syracuse form of $T_{3,c}$ on the odd integers, $\operatorname{Syr}(m)=(3m+c)/2^{\nu_2(3m+c)}$, where $\nu_2$ is the $2$-adic valuation. For $x\ge1$ and odd $m$, the \emph{first passage time} below $x$ is $\tau_x(m)=\inf\{j\ge0:\operatorname{Syr}^j(m)\le x\}$, and the \emph{first passage} is $\operatorname{Pass}_x(m)=\operatorname{Syr}^{\tau_x(m)}(m)$, with $\operatorname{Pass}_x(m)=1$ if $\tau_x(m)=\infty$. Let $\mu(I)=\sum_{m\in I,\ m\text{ odd}}1/m$, so that $\mu([u,v))=\tfrac12\log(v/u)+O(1/u)$. For $1\le u<v$ let $\mathbf{Log}_{u,v}$ be a random odd integer in $[u,v)$ with $\mathbb P(\mathbf{Log}_{u,v}=m)=1/(m\,\mu([u,v)))$, and write $d_{\mathrm{TV}}$ for the total variation distance. The map $n\mapsto n/2$ ($n$ even), $n\mapsto 3n+c$ ($n$ odd) belongs to the class of \cite[Definition~1.2]{GGM_2025} with $p=2$, $q=3$ and $r(1)=c$, and its Syracuse map is $\operatorname{Syr}$; the condition $c\ge-1$ makes it map $\N$ into itself. It satisfies the hypotheses (a)--(c) of \cite[Theorem~1.3]{GGM_2025}: $\gcd(2,3)=1$, $3<2^{2}$, and $3+c$ is even. These hypotheses impose no condition on $\gcd(3,c)$. The proof of \cite[Proposition~3.5]{GGM_2025}, however, is carried out after the normalisation $\gcd(q,r(1))=1$ of \cite[Section~2.2]{GGM_2025}, that is, for $3\nmid c$; Step~0 below reduces Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} to this case.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens}]
\emph{Step 0 (factors $3$ in $c$).} Suppose $3\mid c$, and write $c=3c'$ with $c'\ge1$ odd. Then $T_{3,c}(3m)=3\,T_{3,c'}(m)$ for all $m$, and $T_{3,c}(n)=3(n+c')/2$ for odd $n$. Hence $A'=\{m:3m\in A\}$ is completely invariant under $T_{3,c'}$, and an odd $n$ lies in $A$ if and only if $(n+c')/2\in A'$. Since
\[
\sum_{\substack{n<Y,\ n\text{ odd}\\ (n+c')/2\in A'}}\frac1n=\frac12\sum_{\substack{u<Y/2\\ u\in A'}}\frac1u+O(1),
\]
Step~1 below shows that $\delta(A)$ exists and equals $\delta(A')$ whenever $\delta(A')$ exists. Repeating this step, we may assume $3\nmid c$.

\emph{Input.} For odd $c\ge-1$ with $3\nmid c$, the stabilisation of first passages of Gon\c{c}alves, Greenfeld and Madrid \cite[Proposition~3.5]{GGM_2025}, the analogue of \cite[Proposition~1.11]{Tao_2022}, provides $\varepsilon_0\in(0,1)$ such that for all $\alpha,\lambda\in(1,1+\varepsilon_0)$ and all $x\ge2$
\begin{align}
\mathbb P\bigl(\tau_x(\mathbf{Log}_{x^\lambda,x^{\alpha\lambda}})=\infty\bigr)&\ll x^{-\varepsilon_0},\label{eq:ggm_hit}\\
d_{\mathrm{TV}}\bigl(\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf{Log}_{x^\lambda,x^{\alpha\lambda}}),\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf{Log}_{x^{\alpha\lambda},x^{\alpha^2\lambda}})\bigr)&\ll(\log x)^{-\varepsilon_0},\label{eq:ggm_tv}
\end{align}
with implied constants that do not depend on $x$. Choose $\varepsilon_1>0$ with $(1+\varepsilon_1)^2<1+\varepsilon_0$ and fix $\lambda_0\in(1,1+\varepsilon_1)$.

\emph{Step 1 (odd numbers).} Write $n=2^km$ with $m$ odd. Since $T_{3,c}^k(n)=m$, we have $n\in A$ if and only if $m\in A$. The odd part $A_1=A\cap(2\N+1)$ satisfies $m\in A_1\iff\operatorname{Syr}(m)\in A_1$. Let $\kappa(Y)=\mu(A_1\cap[1,Y))/\mu([1,Y))$. If $\kappa(Y)\to\delta$, then
\[
\sum_{\substack{n<N\\ n\in A}}\frac1n=\sum_{k\ge0}2^{-k}\mu\bigl(A_1\cap[1,N/2^k)\bigr)=\delta\log N+o(\log N),
\]
so $\delta(A)=\delta$. It remains to show that $\kappa(Y)$ converges.

\emph{Step 2 (invariance under first passage).} If $m>x$ and $\tau_x(m)<\infty$, then $m\in A_1\iff\operatorname{Pass}_x(m)\in A_1$. Hence, for every random odd integer $\mathbf m>x$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:passage}
\bigl|\mathbb P(\mathbf m\in A_1)-\mathbb P\bigl(\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf m)\in A_1\bigr)\bigr|\le\mathbb P\bigl(\tau_x(\mathbf m)=\infty\bigr).
\end{equation}

\emph{Step 3 (adjacent windows).} Let $\alpha\in(1,1+\varepsilon_1)$ and $F_\alpha(y)=\mathbb P(\mathbf{Log}_{y,y^\alpha}\in A_1)=\mu(A_1\cap[y,y^\alpha))/\mu([y,y^\alpha))$. For $y\ge4$ put $x=y^{1/\lambda_0}$. The windows $[y,y^\alpha)=[x^{\lambda_0},x^{\alpha\lambda_0})$ and $[y^\alpha,y^{\alpha^2})=[x^{\alpha\lambda_0},x^{\alpha^2\lambda_0})$ lie above $x$, and $\alpha\lambda_0<1+\varepsilon_0$. By~\eqref{eq:passage} for both windows, \eqref{eq:ggm_hit} with $\lambda=\lambda_0$ and $\lambda=\alpha\lambda_0$, and~\eqref{eq:ggm_tv} with $\lambda=\lambda_0$,
\[
|F_\alpha(y)-F_\alpha(y^\alpha)|\ll x^{-\varepsilon_0}+(\log x)^{-\varepsilon_0}\ll(\log y)^{-\varepsilon_0}.
\]

\emph{Step 4 (a limit that is periodic in $\log\log y$).} Put $t=\log\log y$, $a=\log\alpha$ and $f_\alpha(t)=F_\alpha(\exp e^t)$. Then $f_\alpha(t+a)=F_\alpha(y^\alpha)$, so $|f_\alpha(t)-f_\alpha(t+a)|\ll e^{-\varepsilon_0t}$. These differences are summable along $t,t+a,t+2a,\ldots$, so $\psi_\alpha(t)=\lim_{k\to\infty}f_\alpha(t+ka)$ exists, $\psi_\alpha(t+a)=\psi_\alpha(t)$, and $|f_\alpha(t)-\psi_\alpha(t)|\ll e^{-\varepsilon_0t}$. For $W=[y,y^\alpha)$, $W'=[y^{1+\eta},y^{\alpha(1+\eta)})$ and $0<\eta\le1$,
\[
|F_\alpha(y)-F_\alpha(y^{1+\eta})|\le\frac{2\mu(W\triangle W')}{\mu(W)}\le\frac{2(1+\alpha)\eta}{\alpha-1}+O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\log y}\Bigr).
\]
Since the shift in $t$ is $\log(1+\eta)$, this gives $|f_\alpha(t)-f_\alpha(t')|\ll_\alpha|t-t'|+e^{-t}$ for $t\le t'\le t+\log2$, so $\psi_\alpha$ is Lipschitz continuous.

\emph{Step 5 (from windows to $\kappa$).} Let $\tilde\kappa(t)=\kappa(\exp e^t)$ and $h(t)=\mu([1,\exp e^t))=\tfrac12e^t+O(1)$. The identity $\mu(A_1\cap[y,y^\alpha))=F_\alpha(y)\,\mu([y,y^\alpha))$ reads $\tilde\kappa(t+a)h(t+a)=\tilde\kappa(t)h(t)+f_\alpha(t)\bigl(h(t+a)-h(t)\bigr)$, hence
\[
\tilde\kappa(t+a)=e^{-a}\tilde\kappa(t)+(1-e^{-a})f_\alpha(t)+O(e^{-t}).
\]
For fixed $t$, the numbers $D_k=\tilde\kappa(t+ka)-\psi_\alpha(t)$ satisfy $|D_{k+1}|\le e^{-a}|D_k|+O(e^{-\varepsilon_0(t+ka)})$, because $\psi_\alpha(t+ka)=\psi_\alpha(t)$ and $\varepsilon_0<1$. Thus $D_k\to0$ uniformly for $t$ in compact intervals, and $\tilde\kappa(t)-\psi_\alpha(t)\to0$ as $t\to\infty$.

\emph{Step 6 (two incommensurable windows).} Let $\alpha'=\alpha^{1/\sqrt2}$ and $a'=\log\alpha'$. Steps 3--5 for $\alpha'$ give a continuous $a'$-periodic function $\psi_{\alpha'}$ with $\tilde\kappa(t)-\psi_{\alpha'}(t)\to0$, so $\psi_\alpha(t)-\psi_{\alpha'}(t)\to0$. Fix $t$. As $\psi_\alpha(t+ka)=\psi_\alpha(t)$, we get $\psi_{\alpha'}(t+ka)\to\psi_\alpha(t)$ for $k\to\infty$. Since $a/a'=\sqrt2$ is irrational, the points $t+ka$ with $k\ge k_0$ are dense modulo $a'$ for every $k_0$, and $\psi_{\alpha'}$ is continuous; hence $\psi_{\alpha'}$ is constant, equal to $\psi_\alpha(t)$. As $t$ was arbitrary, $\psi_\alpha$ and $\psi_{\alpha'}$ are the same constant $\delta$, and $\kappa(Y)\to\delta$ by Step~5.

Finally, if the natural density of $A$ exists, partial summation shows that the logarithmic density exists with the same value.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Consequences and limits]
\label{rem:consequences}
The set of starting values with unbounded orbits is completely invariant as well, so it also has a logarithmic density. Existence is shared by all maps of the family, in particular by $3x+1$ and $3x-1$; what separates them are the values $\delta(\mathcal C)$. The method does not reach natural densities, because the stabilisation of first passages controls logarithmic averages only. Maps $3x+c$ with $c\le-3$ do not map $\N$ into itself and are not covered; by~\eqref{eq:mirror} they are the maps $3x+|c|$ on the negative integers.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Predecessor sets]
\label{rem:predecessors}
Step~2 uses only that membership is preserved by the first passage below $x$ for all large $x$. This also holds for the set of $n$ whose orbit contains a fixed odd integer $a$, once $x\ge a$: an orbit that starts above $x$ stays above $x$ until its first passage, so it cannot meet $a$ before. Step~1 applies as well, because an orbit can meet the odd number $a$ only after its initial halvings. Hence these sets have logarithmic densities, which answers the existence question of \cite[Remark~1.7]{GGM_2025} in logarithmic form for every odd $a$. Its natural-density form remains open, except for $c=1$ under the hypothesis of Corollary~\ref{cor:natural}, whose proof applies to these sets as well. For $a$ on a cycle, the set is the basin of that cycle.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[A first-passage formula]
\label{rem:passage}
For $3\nmid c$, Steps~2, 4 and~6 and~\eqref{eq:ggm_hit} give, for all $\alpha,\lambda\in(1,1+\varepsilon_1)$,
\[
\delta(\mathcal C)=\lim_{x\to\infty}\mathbb P\bigl(\operatorname{Pass}_x(\mathbf{Log}_{x^{\lambda},x^{\alpha\lambda}})\in\beta(\mathcal C)\bigr).
\]
The density of a basin is the probability that a logarithmically random starting value far above $x$ first enters $[1,x]$ inside that basin. In the proofs of \cite{Tao_2022,GGM_2025}, the law of $\operatorname{Pass}_x$ is controlled through the distribution of the Syracuse offsets modulo powers of $3$. Problem~\ref{prob:basins}(c) thus becomes a question about this $3$-adic law on the small members of each basin.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} does not reach natural counting. For $3x+1$, Mazur \cite{Mazur_2026} has compared the first passage of the uniform law with that of the logarithmic law on the same block. His preprint is not yet refereed, but its proof chain is formalised in Lean~4; we therefore state the consequence as an implication.

\begin{corollary}[Natural densities for $3x+1$]
\label{cor:natural}
Assume the transport estimate \cite[Theorem~3.1]{Mazur_2026}. Then every set $A\subseteq\N$ that is completely invariant under $T_{3,1}$ has a natural density, and it equals $\delta(A)$. In particular, the positive integers whose $3x+1$ orbit reaches $1$ form a set with a natural density.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Let $\alpha=1001/1000$ as in \cite{Mazur_2026}. For large $N$ put $x=N^{1/\alpha^2}$ and $y=x^\alpha$; the odd integers in $[y,y^\alpha]=[N^{1/\alpha},N]$ form a block of \cite[Theorem~3.1]{Mazur_2026}. Let $\mathbf U_y$ be uniform and $\mathbf L_y$ logarithmic on this block. By~\eqref{eq:passage}, by the scheduled no-hit bound and the $\ell^1$ transport bound of \cite[Theorem~3.1]{Mazur_2026}, and by the no-hit bound \cite[(1.18)]{Tao_2022} for $\mathbf L_y$, which \cite{Mazur_2026} uses with this $\alpha$,
\[
\mathbb P(\mathbf U_y\in A_1)=\mathbb P(\mathbf L_y\in A_1)+o(1)\qquad(N\to\infty).
\]
Steps~4 and~6 give $\mathbb P(\mathbf L_y\in A_1)\to\delta(A)$: the window $[y,y^\alpha)$ splits into $K$ consecutive windows of ratio $\alpha^{1/K}<1+\varepsilon_1$, and $\mathbb P(\mathbf L_y\in A_1)$ is their $\mu$-weighted average; the endpoint conventions differ by at most one integer. The odd integers below $N^{1/\alpha}$ form a proportion $O(N^{1/\alpha-1})$ of the odd integers below $N$. Hence $A_1$ has natural density $\delta(A)$ relative to the odd integers. Since $2^km\in A$ if and only if $m\in A$, each set $\{2^km:m\text{ odd}\}$ meets $A$ in the same relative density, and $A$ has natural density $\delta(A)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Natural densities for general $c$]
\label{rem:mazur}
Mazur's argument combines Tao's first-passage architecture with a discrepancy bound for the rotation by $\log_2 3$, which he derives from Rhin's irrationality measure \cite{Mazur_2026}. For $T_{3,c}$ the rotation is the same, and the Syracuse offsets are $c$ times those of $3x+1$: in the notation of \cite{Tao_2022}, $\operatorname{Syr}^n(m)=3^n2^{-|\vec a|}m+c\,F_n(\vec a)$. An analogue of \cite[Theorem~3.1]{Mazur_2026} for $T_{3,c}$ would give natural densities for all basins of all maps $3x+c$ with $c\ge-1$, by the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:natural}, and would settle Problem~\ref{prob:basins}(a). We have not verified that his argument carries over.
\end{remark}

Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens} settles existence in the logarithmic sense. Whether the natural densities exist for $c\ne1$, and nontrivial bounds for their values, remain open.

\subsection*{The landscape of basin densities}
First-passage sampling is cheap enough to survey many maps. For all $133$ maps $3x+c$ with $1\le c\le400$ and $\gcd(c,6)=1$ we sampled $5\cdot10^7$ logarithmic starting values in the window $[10^{30},10^{33}]$. These maps have $956$ cycles, $445$ of them primitive and $126$ with words longer than $40$. Every sampled orbit reached a cycle, the gcd classes have the densities of Proposition~\ref{prop:filtration} within $3.5$ standard errors, and the cycles with words of length at most $40$ are exactly those of Proposition~\ref{prop:rational}. For $3x+97$, $3x+115$, $3x+217$, $3x+241$, $3x+311$ and $3x+383$ this holds for both primitive cycles, which therefore attract exactly one half of the integers prime to $c$ each. Simple features of a cycle explain much less. Within the maps, the share does not depend on the size of the cycle minimum (sign test over $98$ maps, $p=0.22$), and it increases only weakly with the word length ($p=0.002$). Pooled over all maps, both features seem to matter, but such correlations mix maps with few and with many cycles.

\paragraph{Residue classes.} Beyond Propositions~\ref{prop:filtration} and~\ref{prop:cosets}, residue classes carry no information about the basin. We drew the starting values from up to $64$ fixed classes modulo $2^k$, $k=4,\dots,30$, and modulo $3^j$, $j=1,\dots,12$, with $10^6$ values per class, for $3x-1$, $3x+5$ and $3x+13$. The class shares agree within the sampling error. In the first run the $36$ chi-square tests gave $p$-values between $0.03$ and $1.00$; the extreme values for $3x+13$ did not recur in a repetition with new classes, and with $10^7$ values per class the classes modulo $9$ and $27$ differ from the pooled shares by at most $3\cdot10^{-4}$. The classes modulo $5$ for $3x+5$ and modulo $13$ for $3x+13$ served as positive controls. This quantifies Table~\ref{table:classes}. A class modulo $2^k$ fixes the first $k$ parities and is mapped onto a progression modulo a power of $3$, and the $3$-adic mixing of the Syracuse offsets \cite{Tao_2022} explains, heuristically, why the class leaves no trace in the basin shares.

\subsection*{A predictor from the backward tree}
For a cycle $\mathcal C$ of $T_{3,c}$, let level $0$ consist of the predecessors of its members that are not on $\mathcal C$, and level $d+1$ of the predecessors of level $d$: the number $2y$, and $(2y-c)/3$ if this is a positive integer, which is then odd. Nodes divisible by $3$ have no odd predecessor; their subtrees are doubling chains of density $0$, and we do not expand them. Let $M_d(\mathcal C)$ be the sum of $1/x$ over the nodes $x\ge c/2$ of level $d$ that are not divisible by $3$. Below $c/2$ a node has no odd predecessor either, and counting it would only add transient spikes. The even predecessor of a node $y$ carries $1/(2y)$; an odd predecessor exists for half of the nodes, is counted with probability $2/3$ and carries $3/(2y)$. On average, therefore, the mass is the same on every level, and heuristically its average over deep levels is proportional to $\delta(\mathcal C)$, with a factor that depends only on the map.

We computed $M_d$ up to depth $70$ for the $410$ primitive cycles of the $98$ maps of Figure~\ref{fig:landscape} with at least two primitive cycles, by a depth-first search that needs no memory, and normalised the mean mass of the levels $64$ to $69$ within every map. The prediction reproduces the measured shares with a median deviation of $4.9\,\%$, within a factor of $1.25$ for $90\,\%$ of the cycles, and it identifies the largest basin in $87$ of the $98$ maps (Figure~\ref{fig:predictor}). Shares proportional to the word length, or to the mass of level $0$ alone, miss by a factor of about $2$ in the median. The error decreases steadily with the depth, without a plateau so far (Table~\ref{table:convergence}). Normalising the prediction within the cosets of Proposition~\ref{prop:cosets} lowers the median deviation to $3.3\,\%$, and the largest basin is then correct in $91$ maps.

Why the averages of $M_d$ approach the basin densities, and whether they converge to them, is open. This is the precise form of Problem~\ref{prob:basins}(c).

\begin{problem}
\label{prob:basins}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:filtration}, the basin densities of $3x+c$ are determined by those of the cycles coprime to $c'$ in the maps $3x+c'$, $c'\mid c$. For these cycles:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
	\item \emph{Natural density.} Do the limits $\lim_{N\to\infty}d_N(\mathcal C)$ exist? If so, they equal $\delta(\mathcal C)$ by Theorem~\ref{thm:logdens}. For $c=1$ they exist under the hypothesis of Corollary~\ref{cor:natural}; Remark~\ref{rem:mazur} outlines a route for general $c$. Table~\ref{table:passage} shows no difference between natural and logarithmic counting up to $10^{220}$.
	\item \emph{Explanation.} Proposition~\ref{prop:cosets} fixes the densities of the cycles that are alone in their coset. What decides between the cycles of one coset: their parity words, the arithmetic position of their members, the distribution of their backward iterates?
	\item \emph{Prediction.} Do the level averages of the backward mass $M_d(\mathcal C)$ converge to $\delta(\mathcal C)$, up to a factor that depends only on the map? Numerically they reproduce the measured values to about $5\,\%$ (Table~\ref{table:convergence}).
\end{enumerate}
By Corollary~\ref{cor:local}, any answer to (b) and (c) must use global information. For $3x+1$, the conjecture asserts a single basin of density $1$.
\end{problem}



%=====================================================================
\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:outlook}
%=====================================================================
The local and the global view answer different questions. Cayley trees and their congruence classes describe how every forest of the family branches. Parity words and the numbers $2^l-q^k$ decide how many trees a forest has and where their cycles sit. We see five directions, in order of priority:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
	\item \emph{The values of the basin densities} (Problem~\ref{prob:basins}). Existence is settled in the logarithmic sense, Proposition~\ref{prop:cosets} fixes some values exactly, and the backward mass predicts the others to about $5\,\%$. The central open question is why: a proof that the level averages of the backward mass converge to the densities would turn this heuristic into a formula. Remark~\ref{rem:passage} expresses each density as a first-passage probability, which may be the route to such a proof and already allows numerical estimates far beyond brute-force enumeration (Table~\ref{table:passage}). Further aims are natural densities, most directly through an analogue of Mazur's natural-counting transport for $T_{3,c}$ (Remark~\ref{rem:mazur}), and the features of a cycle that determine the values. This is the direction with the largest potential for new theory, independently of the Collatz conjecture itself.
	\item \emph{The arithmetic structure of the primitive cycles} (Problem~\ref{prob:heuristic}): why the equidistribution heuristic works and why the counts are underdispersed.
	\item \emph{Longer words}: extending Proposition~\ref{prop:rational} to $l\le44$ and beyond, and the role of the convergents of $\log_2 3$, such as $65/41$.
	\item \emph{A Steiner-type theorem for Ba\v{r}ina's $7x\pm1$} \cite{Barina_2022}: no non-trivial circuits, via Diophantine approximation of $\log_2 7$ and Baker-type bounds, in the spirit of \cite{Steiner_1977,Simons_deWeger_2005}.
	\item \emph{Certification}: a Lean~4 formalisation of Lemma~\ref{lem:words} and Propositions~\ref{prop:unique_parent}, \ref{prop:local} and~\ref{prop:filtration} that refers to the maps themselves.
\end{enumerate}
